Markov (Markovian) Process¶

A Markovian process (or Markov process) is a stochastic process in which the future evolution depends only on the present state, not on the history of how the system got there.

A stochastic process {Xt}\{X_t\}{Xt​} is Markovian if it satisfies the Markov property:

P(Xt+1=x∣Xt,Xt−1,…,X0)=P(Xt+1=x∣Xt).P(X_{t+1} = x \mid X_t, X_{t-1}, \ldots, X_0) = P(X_{t+1} = x \mid X_t).P(Xt+1​=x∣Xt​,Xt−1​,…,X0​)=P(Xt+1​=x∣Xt​).
(1)

where PPP is the transition probability.

  • Memoryless: future depends only on the present.

  • Described by transition matrices (discrete process) or transition rates (continuous process).

  • The system evolution is probabilistic, not deterministic.

  • The process typically assumes short correlation times.

Discrete Markov process¶

A discrete-time Markov process has states

S={1,2,…,N}.S = \{1,2,\dots,N\}.S={1,2,…,N}.
(2)

[Transition matrix]{style=“color: blue”} P=(Pij)\mathbf{P} = (P_{ij})P=(Pij​) is defined by:

  • Pij≥0P_{ij} \ge 0Pij​≥0,

  • ∑j=1NPij=1\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^N P_{ij} = 1j=1∑N​Pij​=1 for every state iii,

  • Pij=Pr⁡[Xt+1=j∣Xt=i]P_{ij} = \Pr[X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i]Pij​=Pr[Xt+1​=j∣Xt​=i] (probability of going from state iii to state jjj)

Each row of P\mathbf{P}P is a probability distribution describing how the system evolves in one time step.

Markov Property in Matrix Form¶

The current [probability distribution]{style=“color: blue”} over states is a row vector:

π(t)=(Pr⁡[Xt=1]⋯Pr⁡[Xt=N])\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \Pr[X_t=1] & \cdots & \Pr[X_t=N] \end{pmatrix}π(t)=(Pr[Xt​=1]​⋯​Pr[Xt​=N]​)
(3)

Then the next-step distribution is:

π(t+1)=π(t) P.\boldsymbol{\pi}(t+1) = \boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \, \mathbf{P}.π(t+1)=π(t)P.
(4)

After nnn steps:

π(t+n)=π(t) Pn.\boldsymbol{\pi}(t+n) = \boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \, \mathbf{P}^n.π(t+n)=π(t)Pn.
(5)
Note

Interpretation: Pn\mathbf{P}^nPn gives nnn-step transition probabilities.

Example of a Transition Matrix¶

Consider a 3-state Markov chain. The transition matrix is:

P=(0.60.30.10.20.50.30.00.40.6).\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.5 & 0.3 \\ 0.0 & 0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}.P=⎝⎛​0.60.20.0​0.30.50.4​0.10.30.6​⎠⎞​.
(6)

Interpretation of the first row (transition probabilities for State 1):

  • Probability to stay: 0.6 stay,

  • Probability to move to State 2: 0.3,

  • Probability to move to State 3: 0.1.

Rows sum to 1, as required for a stochastic matrix.

Two-Step and Multi-Step Transitions¶

The probability of being in state jjj after two steps from iii is:

Pij2=∑k=1NPikPkj.\mathbf{P}^2_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^N P_{ik} P_{kj}.Pij2​=k=1∑N​Pik​Pkj​.
(7)

Thus:

  • P2\mathbf{P}^2P2: all 2-step transition probabilities,

  • P3\mathbf{P}^3P3: all 3-step transition probabilities,

  • Pn\mathbf{P}^nPn: nnn-step transition probabilities.

Markov chains propagate by matrix powers:

π(n)=π(0)Pn.\boldsymbol{\pi}(n) = \boldsymbol{\pi}(0) \mathbf{P}^n.π(n)=π(0)Pn.
(8)

Stationary Distribution¶

A stationary distribution π∗\boldsymbol{\pi}^*π∗ satisfies:

π∗=π∗P,∑iπi∗=1.\boldsymbol{\pi}^* = \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \mathbf{P}, \qquad \sum_i \pi^*_i = 1.π∗=π∗P,i∑​πi∗​=1.
(9)

Intuitively, the stationary distribution provides the long-term probability of being in each state.

Properties:

  • Represents the long-term behavior of the Markov chain.

  • If chain is irreducible (i.e., all states communicate) and aperiodic (i.e., no deterministic cycles), then:

lim⁡n→∞Pn=(π∗π∗⋮π∗).\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbf{P}^n = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \\ \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \end{pmatrix}.n→∞lim​Pn=⎝⎛​π∗π∗⋮π∗​⎠⎞​.
(10)

  • The stationary distribution is unique under these conditions.

1D Random Walk as a Markov Process¶

A simple model of diffusion or stochastic motion:

Xn+1=Xn+ηn,X_{n+1} = X_n + \eta_n,Xn+1​=Xn​+ηn​,
(11)

where

ηn={+1with probability p,−1with probability q=1−p.\eta_n = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{with probability } p, \\ -1 & \text{with probability } q = 1-p. \end{cases}ηn​={+1−1​with probability p,with probability q=1−p.​
(12)

Markov property:

P(Xn+1=x∣Xn)=P(ηn=x−Xn).P(X_{n+1} = x \mid X_n) = P(\eta_n = x-X_n).P(Xn+1​=x∣Xn​)=P(ηn​=x−Xn​).
(13)

Properties:

  • Mean displacement: ⟨Xn⟩=(p−q) n\langle X_n \rangle = (p-q)\, n⟨Xn​⟩=(p−q)n.

  • Variance: Var(Xn)=4pq n\mathrm{Var}(X_n) = 4pq\, nVar(Xn​)=4pqn.

  • In the continuum limit: leads to the diffusion equation.

Applications:

  • molecular diffusion,

  • polymer models,

  • financial models (stock price steps)

Monte-Carlo method¶

Monte-Carlo (MC) methods compute quantities using random sampling.

  • Evaluate integrals in high dimensions.

  • Sample probability distributions.

  • Estimate thermodynamic and statistical averages.

Key idea:

⟨A⟩=1N∑i=1NA(xi),\langle A \rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} A(x_i),⟨A⟩=N1​i=1∑N​A(xi​),
(14)

where the xix_ixi​ are random samples.

Monte-Carlo in molecular simulations¶

  • The task is to explore the molecular configurational space rN\mathbf{r}^NrN and evaluate average thermodynamic properties.

  • Let’s consider average potential energy as an example:

⟨V(rN)⟩=∫drNV(rN)ρ(rN)\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \int d\mathbf{r}^N V(\mathbf{r}^N) \rho(\mathbf{r}^N)⟨V(rN)⟩=∫drNV(rN)ρ(rN)
(15)

ρ(rN)\rho(\mathbf{r}^N)ρ(rN) is the probability of obtaining the configuration rN\mathbf{r}^NrN:

ρ(rN)=exp⁡[−V(rN)/kBT]Z,\rho(\mathbf{r}^N) = \frac{\exp[-V(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{Z},ρ(rN)=Zexp[−V(rN)/kB​T]​,
(16)

ZZZ is the configurational integral

Z=∫drNexp⁡(−V(rN)kBT)Z = \int d\mathbf{r}^N \exp \left( -\frac{V(\mathbf{r}^N)}{k_BT} \right)Z=∫drNexp(−kB​TV(rN)​)
(17)

Monte-Carlo in molecular simulations (continued)¶

Let’s try to calculate the potential energy integral using a simple Monte Carlo method. This will result in the following steps:

  1. Obtain a configuration of the system by randomly generating 3N Cartesian coordinates, which are assigned to the particles.

  2. Calculate the potential energy of the configuration, V(rN)V(\mathbf{r}^N)V(rN).

  3. From the potential energy, calculate the Boltzmann factor, exp⁡[−V(rN)/kBT]\exp[-V(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]exp[−V(rN)/kB​T].

  4. Add the Boltzmann factor to the accumulated sum of Boltzmann factors and the potential energy contribution to its accumulated sum and return to step 1.

  5. After a number, NtrialN_{trial}Ntrial​, of iterations, the mean value of the potential energy would be calculated using:

⟨V(rN)⟩=∑i=1NtrialVi(rN)exp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]∑i=1Ntrialexp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}} V_i(\mathbf{r}^N) \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}} \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}⟨V(rN)⟩=∑i=1Ntrial​​exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T]∑i=1Ntrial​​Vi​(rN)exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T]​
(18)

Monte-Carlo in molecular simulations (continued)¶

⟨V(rN)⟩=∑i=1NtrialVi(rN)exp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]∑i=1Ntrialexp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}} V_i(\mathbf{r}^N) \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}} \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}⟨V(rN)⟩=∑i=1Ntrial​​exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T]∑i=1Ntrial​​Vi​(rN)exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T]​
(19)

Problem: The large number (most!) of configurations have extremely small (effectively zero) Boltzmann factors caused by high-energy overlaps between the particles. Only a very small proportion of the phase space corresponds to low-energy configurations where there are no overlapping particles and where the Boltzmann factor has an appreciable value. These low-energy regions coincide with the physically observed phases such as solid, liquid, etc.

Solution:

  • Let’s try to generate configurations that make a large contribution to the integral.

  • This is the strategy adopted in importance sampling, which is the essence of the Metropolis Monte Carlo introduced in 1953.

Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC)¶

MCMC constructs a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the target distribution π(x)\pi(x)π(x). Typical scheme:

  1. From current state xxx, propose a move x′x'x′.

  2. Accept or reject this move based on transition rules.

  3. Repeat to generate samples.

The chain must be:

  • [Ergodic]{style=“color: blue”}

  • Aperiodic

  • Satisfy [detailed balance]{style=“color: blue”}

π(x)P(x→x′)=π(x′)P(x′→x).\pi(x) P(x \to x') = \pi(x') P(x' \to x).π(x)P(x→x′)=π(x′)P(x′→x).
(20)

Ergodicity in Markov Chains¶

A Markov chain is [ergodic]{style=“color: blue”} if it satisfies:

  1. Irreducibility: every state can be reached from any other state,

  2. Aperiodicity: the system does not get trapped in deterministic cycles

  3. Positive recurrence: expected return time to each state is finite.

Consequences of ergodicity:

  • A unique stationary distribution π∗\boldsymbol{\pi}^*π∗ exists.

  • [Long-term time averages equal ensemble averages]{style=“color: blue”}:

lim⁡n→∞1n∑k=1nf(Xk)=∑iπi∗f(i)=⟨f⟩.\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n f(X_k) = \sum_i \pi^*_i f(i) = \langle f \rangle.n→∞lim​n1​k=1∑n​f(Xk​)=i∑​πi∗​f(i)=⟨f⟩.
(21)

  • In a continuous-time ergodic Markov process X(t), this becomes:

lim⁡T→∞1T∫0∞f(X(T))dt=∫f(x)π(x)dx=⟨f⟩\lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^\infty f(X(T))dt = \int f(x) \pi(x) dx = \langle f \rangleT→∞lim​T1​∫0∞​f(X(T))dt=∫f(x)π(x)dx=⟨f⟩
(22)

  • MCMC simulations converge to the correct equilibrium distribution.

Detailed Balance: Intuition¶

Key Question: How can we ensure that a Markov chain samples from a desired distribution π\piπ?

Idea: In equilibrium, probability flow between any two states is symmetric:

Flow(i→j)=Flow(j→i)\text{Flow}(i \to j) = \text{Flow}(j \to i)Flow(i→j)=Flow(j→i)
(23)

Interpretation:

  • At equilibrium, every microscopic transition is balanced by its reverse transition.

  • No net probability currents.

  • System appears [“reversible”]{style=“color: blue”} at the level of state transitions.

Consequence: If this condition holds, π\piπ is the stationary distribution of the chain.

Formal Definition of Detailed Balance¶

A Markov chain with transition matrix PPP satisfies detailed balance with respect to distribution π\piπ if

πiPij=πjPji∀i,j.\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji} \quad \forall i,j.πi​Pij​=πj​Pji​∀i,j.
(24)

Meaning:

  • πiPij\pi_i P_{ij}πi​Pij​ = probability of being in state iii and moving to jjj.

  • πjPji\pi_j P_{ji}πj​Pji​ = probability of being in jjj and moving to iii.

  • Equality ensures reversible microscopic dynamics.

This is a stronger condition than merely having πP=π\pi \mathbf{P} = \piπP=π.

Detailed Balance Implies Stationarity¶

Given detailed balance:

πiPij=πjPji,\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji},πi​Pij​=πj​Pji​,
(25)

Sum over all iii:

∑iπiPij=πj∑iPji.\sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j \sum_i P_{ji}.i∑​πi​Pij​=πj​i∑​Pji​.
(26)

But since columns of PPP sum to 1: ∑iPji=1\sum_i P_{ji} = 1∑i​Pji​=1,
we get:

∑iπiPij=πj.\sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j.i∑​πi​Pij​=πj​.
(27)

Therefore:

πP=π,\pi \mathbf{P} = \pi,πP=π,
(28)

so π\piπ is the stationary distribution.

Conclusion: A reversible chain automatically has the correct equilibrium distribution.

Reversibility and Microscopic Interpretation¶

Detailed balance is equivalent to [time reversibility]{style=“color: blue”}:

πiPij=πjPji\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}πi​Pij​=πj​Pji​
(29)

means that the joint probability of observing the transition

i→ji \to ji→j
(30)

is the same as observing

j→i.j \to i.j→i.
(31)

Physical interpretation (statistical mechanics):

  • Detailed balance corresponds to microscopic reversibility.

  • In equilibrium, every elementary process is balanced by its reverse.

  • No net entropy production at the transition level.

Visual Interpretation¶

Detailed balance = equality of flows

πiPij=πjPji.\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}.πi​Pij​=πj​Pji​.
(32)

This condition prevents probability currents from circulating and ensures reversibility.

Detailed Balance and Markov Chain Monte Carlo¶

Most Monte Carlo algorithms explicitly enforce detailed balance so that π\piπ is the correct sampling distribution.

Note

Examples:

  • Metropolis Algorithm Ensures

πiqijaij=πjqjiaji\pi_i q_{ij} a_{ij} = \pi_j q_{ji} a_{ji}πi​qij​aij​=πj​qji​aji​
(33)

where qijq_{ij}qij​ is proposal probability and aija_{ij}aij​ acceptance probability.

  • Metropolis--Hastings updates are constructed to satisfy detailed balance.

  • Gibbs sampling automatically satisfies detailed balance.

  • Replica exchange MC/MD satisfies detailed balance across temperatures.

When Detailed Balance is Not Required¶

Although very common, detailed balance is not necessary for correct sampling. The weaker condition is:

πP=π.\pi \mathbf{P} = \pi.πP=π.
(34)

Why avoid detailed balance?

  • Non-reversible chains often mix faster.

  • Can reduce autocorrelation times in MCMC.

  • Used in modern algorithms (e.g., lifted MCMC, non-reversible Langevin).

Takeaway: Detailed balance is convenient, intuitive, and widely used---but not mandatory.

Metropolis Monte Carlo¶

In Metropolis Monte Carlo:

Pij=q(i→j) a(i→j),i≠j,P_{ij} = q(i \to j)\, a(i \to j), \quad i \ne j,Pij​=q(i→j)a(i→j),i=j,
(35)

where:

  • q(i→j)q(i \to j)q(i→j): proposal distribution,

  • a(i→j)a(i \to j)a(i→j): Metropolis acceptance probability,

a(i→j)=min⁡(1,π(j)q(j→i)π(i)q(i→j)).a(i \to j) = \min\left(1,\frac{\pi(j) q(j\to i)}{\pi(i) q(i \to j)}\right).a(i→j)=min(1,π(i)q(i→j)π(j)q(j→i)​).
(36)

Diagonal elements:

Pii=1−∑j≠iPij.P_{ii} = 1 - \sum_{j\ne i} P_{ij}.Pii​=1−j=i∑​Pij​.
(37)

Guarantees:

  • rows sum to 1,

  • detailed balance,

  • convergence to target distribution π\piπ.

Metropolis Algorithm: Step-by-Step¶

Given a target probability distribution π(x)∝e−βE(x)\pi(x) \propto e^{-\beta E(x)}π(x)∝e−βE(x) (e.g., Boltzmann weight):

  1. Start from an initial state xxx.

  2. Propose a new state x′x'x′ from a proposal distribution q(x→x′)q(x \to x')q(x→x′).

  3. Compute the Metropolis acceptance ratio:

α=min⁡(1,  π(x′)q(x′→x)π(x)q(x→x′)).\alpha = \min\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(x') q(x' \to x)}{\pi(x) q(x \to x')}\right).α=min(1,π(x)q(x→x′)π(x′)q(x′→x)​).
(38)

  1. Accept the new state with probability α\alphaα:

xn+1={x′,with probability α,x,otherwise.x_{n+1} = \begin{cases} x', & \text{with probability } \alpha,\\ x, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}xn+1​={x′,x,​with probability α,otherwise.​
(39)

  1. Iterate to generate a correlated trajectory sampling π(x)\pi(x)π(x).

Effect:

  • Energy downhill moves (toward higher probability) are always accepted.

  • Energy uphill moves are accepted with the appropriate probability.

Metropolis Monte-Carlo in Molecular Modeling¶

Again, we are up to computing this 3N3N3N-dimensional integral:

⟨V(rN)⟩=∑i=1NtrialVi(rN)exp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]∑i=1Ntrialexp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}} V_i(\mathbf{r}^N) \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}} \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}⟨V(rN)⟩=∑i=1Ntrial​​exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T]∑i=1Ntrial​​Vi​(rN)exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T]​
(40)

  • The crucial feature of the Metropolis approach is that it biases the generation of configurations towards those that make the most significant contribution to the integral.

  • Specifically, it generates states with a probability exp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]\exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T] and then counts each of them equally.

  • By contrast, the simple Monte Carlo integration method generates states with equal probability (both high- and low-energy) and then assigns them a weight exp⁡[−Vi(rN)/kBT]\exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]exp[−Vi​(rN)/kB​T].

  • In this way, Metropolis MC avoids explicit integration over 3N3N3N-dimensional space.

Metropolis MC: Proposal and Acceptance¶

Center

Acceptance probability:

A(i→j)=min⁡(1,π(j) q(j→i)π(i) q(i→j))A(i \to j) = \min\Bigg(1, \frac{\pi(j)\, q(j \to i)}{\pi(i)\, q(i \to j)} \Bigg)A(i→j)=min(1,π(i)q(i→j)π(j)q(j→i)​)
(41)

Explanation:

  • π(i)\pi(i)π(i) = target probability (e.g., Boltzmann factor e−βEie^{-\beta E_i}e−βEi​)

  • q(i→j)q(i \to j)q(i→j) = probability of proposing jjj from iii

  • If accepted: move to jjj, otherwise stay at iii

  • Ensures detailed balance and convergence to π\piπ

Proposal Distributions¶

The proposal distribution q(i→j)q(i \to j)q(i→j) determines how new states are suggested in Metropolis MC:

  • Symmetric proposals:

    • q(i→j)=q(j→i)q(i \to j) = q(j \to i)q(i→j)=q(j→i)

    • Example: uniform step to a neighbor

    • Acceptance ratio simplifies to A=min⁡(1,π(j)/π(i))A = \min(1, \pi(j)/\pi(i))A=min(1,π(j)/π(i))

  • Asymmetric proposals:

    • q(i→j)≠q(j→i)q(i \to j) \neq q(j \to i)q(i→j)=q(j→i)

    • Example: biased random walk toward higher probability regions

    • Acceptance ratio includes q(j→i)/q(i→j)q(j \to i)/q(i \to j)q(j→i)/q(i→j) factor

  • Discrete proposals:

    • Pick a new state randomly from a finite set

    • Example: random change of a single atom coordinate

  • Problem-specific proposals:

    • Use system knowledge to propose moves efficiently

    • Example: block updates, cluster moves, Hamiltonian proposals

Goal: Choose qqq to explore the state space efficiently while ensuring detailed balance.
