Lagrange Multipliers: An Inclined-Plane Example

Purdue University
Course-material provenance

This page was migrated from the CHM 696 course materials developed by Jonathan Hood and Lyudmila Slipchenko. Equations and examples remain under editorial review during the Fall 2026 website migration.

Learning goals¶

After this lecture, you should be able to:

  • encode a holonomic constraint with a Lagrange multiplier;

  • derive the constrained Euler--Lagrange equations;

  • eliminate the multiplier to recover the physical motion; and

  • relate the multiplier to the geometric constraint force.

Setup¶

Consider a particle (block) of mass mmm moving in the xyxyxy--plane. The straight line (inclined by angle α\alphaα to the xxx--axis) is given by the holonomic constraint

ϕ(x,y)=y−xtan⁡α=0.\phi(x,y)=y - x\tan\alpha = 0.ϕ(x,y)=y−xtanα=0.
(1)

Take yyy positive upward and gravity g=(0,−g)\mathbf{g}=(0,-g)g=(0,−g). Assume no friction.

Kinetic and potential energies are

T=12m(x˙2+y˙2),V=mgy.T=\tfrac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2),\qquad V=m g y.T=21​m(x˙2+y˙​2),V=mgy.
(2)

Form the augmented Lagrangian with multiplier λ\lambdaλ:

L=T−V+λϕ=12m(x˙2+y˙2)−mgy+λ (y−xtan⁡α).\mathcal{L}=T-V+\lambda\phi = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)-m g y + \lambda\,(y - x\tan\alpha).L=T−V+λϕ=21​m(x˙2+y˙​2)−mgy+λ(y−xtanα).
(3)

Euler--Lagrange equations with a multiplier¶

Treating x,yx,yx,y as independent coordinates, the Euler--Lagrange equations give

ddt(mx˙)−∂L∂x=0⟹mx¨+λtan⁡α=0,ddt(my˙)−∂L∂y=0⟹my¨+mg−λ=0,\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot x)-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\ddot x + \lambda \tan\alpha = 0,\\[6pt] &\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot y)-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial y}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\ddot y + m g - \lambda = 0, \end{aligned}​dtd​(mx˙)−∂x∂L​=0⟹mx¨+λtanα=0,dtd​(my˙​)−∂y∂L​=0⟹my¨​+mg−λ=0,​
(4)

supplemented by the constraint

y−xtan⁡α=0.y - x\tan\alpha = 0.y−xtanα=0.
(5)

With this sign convention, λ∇ϕ\lambda\nabla\phiλ∇ϕ is the constraint force in Cartesian coordinates. The numerical value of λ\lambdaλ depends on how ϕ\phiϕ is normalized, so it is not automatically the physical normal-force magnitude.

Eliminate the multiplier¶

Introduce the coordinate sss measured along the line (positive in the direction making angle α\alphaα with the xxx--axis). The unit vector along the line is

u=(cos⁡α,sin⁡α),\mathbf{u}=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha),u=(cosα,sinα),
(6)

so the position on the line is r=su\mathbf{r}=s\mathbf{u}r=su, hence

x=scos⁡α,y=ssin⁡α,x = s\cos\alpha,\qquad y = s\sin\alpha,x=scosα,y=ssinα,
(7)

and

x¨=s¨cos⁡α,y¨=s¨sin⁡α.\ddot x = \ddot s\cos\alpha,\qquad \ddot y = \ddot s\sin\alpha.x¨=s¨cosα,y¨​=s¨sinα.
(8)

From the xxx--equation:

mx¨+λtan⁡α=0⇒ms¨cos⁡α+λsin⁡αcos⁡α=0m\ddot x + \lambda\tan\alpha = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad m\ddot s\cos\alpha + \lambda\frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}=0mx¨+λtanα=0⇒ms¨cosα+λcosαsinα​=0
(9)

or

ms¨cos⁡2α+λsin⁡α=0.(1)m\ddot s\cos^2\alpha + \lambda\sin\alpha = 0. \tag{1}ms¨cos2α+λsinα=0.(1)
(10)

From the yyy--equation:

my¨+mg−λ=0⇒ms¨sin⁡α+mg−λ=0,m\ddot y + m g - \lambda = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad m\ddot s\sin\alpha + m g - \lambda = 0,my¨​+mg−λ=0⇒ms¨sinα+mg−λ=0,
(11)

so

λ=ms¨sin⁡α+mg.(2)\lambda = m\ddot s\sin\alpha + m g. \tag{2}λ=ms¨sinα+mg.(2)
(12)

Substitute (2) into (1):

ms¨cos⁡2α+(ms¨sin⁡α+mg)sin⁡α=0.m\ddot s\cos^2\alpha + (m\ddot s\sin\alpha + m g)\sin\alpha = 0.ms¨cos2α+(ms¨sinα+mg)sinα=0.
(13)

Divide by mmm and use cos⁡2α+sin⁡2α=1\cos^2\alpha+\sin^2\alpha=1cos2α+sin2α=1 to obtain

s¨+gsin⁡α=0,\ddot s + g\sin\alpha = 0,s¨+gsinα=0,
(14)

hence

s¨=− gsin⁡α.\boxed{\ddot s = -\,g\sin\alpha.}s¨=−gsinα.​
(15)

Thus the acceleration along the line (taking sss positive upslope) is −gsin⁡α-g\sin\alpha−gsinα, i.e. the particle accelerates downhill with magnitude gsin⁡αg\sin\alphagsinα.

Normal reaction¶

From (2)(2)(2) we get

λ=ms¨sin⁡α+mg=m(−gsin⁡α)sin⁡α+mg=mg(1−sin⁡2α)=mgcos⁡2α.\lambda = m\ddot s\sin\alpha + m g = m(-g\sin\alpha)\sin\alpha + m g = m g(1-\sin^2\alpha) = m g\cos^2\alpha.λ=ms¨sinα+mg=m(−gsinα)sinα+mg=mg(1−sin2α)=mgcos2α.
(16)

The actual normal force magnitude (geometric normal) is

N=λcos⁡α=mgcos⁡α,N=\frac{\lambda}{\cos\alpha}=m g\cos\alpha,N=cosαλ​=mgcosα,
(17)

which is the familiar normal reaction on an inclined line.

Summary¶

The constrained Euler--Lagrange system is

{mx¨+λtan⁡α=0,my¨+mg−λ=0,y−xtan⁡α=0.\begin{cases} m\ddot x + \lambda \tan\alpha = 0,\\[4pt] m\ddot y + m g - \lambda = 0,\\[4pt] y - x\tan\alpha = 0. \end{cases}⎩⎨⎧​mx¨+λtanα=0,my¨​+mg−λ=0,y−xtanα=0.​
(18)

Eliminating λ\lambdaλ and using the coordinate sss along the line yields

s¨=−gsin⁡α,λ=mgcos⁡2α,\ddot s = -g\sin\alpha,\qquad \lambda = m g\cos^2\alpha,s¨=−gsinα,λ=mgcos2α,
(19)

and the physical normal force on the block is N=mgcos⁡αN=mg\cos\alphaN=mgcosα.

Geometry and force decomposition on the incline¶

One annotated diagram connects the coordinate constraint, the along-slope direction, gravity, and the normal reaction used in the multiplier derivation.

alpha = np.deg2rad(28)
tangent = np.array([np.cos(alpha), np.sin(alpha)])
normal = np.array([-np.sin(alpha), np.cos(alpha)])
center = 2.5*tangent + 0.28*normal

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.3, 5.0))
x = np.linspace(0,5.2,100)
ax.plot(x, x*np.tan(alpha), color=BLACK, linewidth=3)
block = Rectangle((-0.38,-0.23), 0.76, 0.46, facecolor=GOLD,
                  edgecolor=BLACK, linewidth=1.5)
block.set_transform(Affine2D().rotate(alpha).translate(*center) + ax.transData)
ax.add_patch(block)
ax.arrow(*center, 0, -1.25, width=0.035, head_width=0.18, head_length=0.16,
         color=BLACK, length_includes_head=True)
ax.arrow(*center, *(1.1*normal), width=0.035, head_width=0.18, head_length=0.16,
         color=DARK_GOLD, length_includes_head=True)
ax.arrow(*center, *(-0.82*tangent), width=0.028, head_width=0.15, head_length=0.14,
         color=GRAY, length_includes_head=True)
ax.text(*(center + np.array([0.15,-0.75])), r"$m\mathbf{g}$")
ax.text(*(center + 0.65*normal + np.array([0.12,0.05])), "$N$")
ax.text(*(center - 0.55*tangent - 0.25*normal), r"$mg\sin\alpha$")
ax.add_patch(Arc((0,0), 1.5, 1.5, theta1=0, theta2=np.degrees(alpha),
                 color=DARK_GOLD, linewidth=2))
ax.text(0.85, 0.16, r"$\alpha$")
ax.set(aspect="equal", xlim=(-0.2,5.5), ylim=(-0.4,3.4),
       xlabel="$x$", ylabel="$y$", title="The multiplier force is normal to the constraint")
plt.show()

The gradient of the constraint supplies the normal direction¶

Contours of ϕ(x,y)=y−xtan⁡α\phi(x,y)=y-x\tan\alphaϕ(x,y)=y−xtanα are parallel lines. Its gradient is perpendicular to every contour, so λ∇ϕ\lambda\nabla\phiλ∇ϕ has exactly the geometry required of a constraint force.

alpha = np.deg2rad(32)
x = np.linspace(-1,4,250)
y = np.linspace(-1,3.2,220)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x,y)
Phi = Y - X*np.tan(alpha)
point = np.array([2.2, 2.2*np.tan(alpha)])
gradient = np.array([-np.tan(alpha), 1.0])
gradient = gradient / np.linalg.norm(gradient)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.2, 4.8))
contours = ax.contour(X,Y,Phi,levels=[-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1],colors=GRAY,linewidths=1)
ax.clabel(contours, inline=True, fmt=lambda value: rf"$\phi={value:g}$")
ax.contour(X,Y,Phi,levels=[0],colors=[BLACK],linewidths=3)
ax.scatter(*point, color=GOLD, edgecolor=BLACK, s=70, zorder=3)
ax.arrow(*point, *(1.15*gradient), width=0.035, head_width=0.18,
         head_length=0.16, color=DARK_GOLD, length_includes_head=True)
ax.text(*(point + 0.72*gradient + np.array([0.12,0.05])), r"$\nabla\phi$")
ax.set(aspect="equal", xlim=(-1,4), ylim=(-1,3.2), xlabel="$x$", ylabel="$y$",
       title="The constraint gradient is normal to the allowed path")
plt.show()

Incline angle trades acceleration against normal force¶

Plotting both dimensionless quantities against angle gives immediate limiting checks: a horizontal plane has maximum normal force and no downhill acceleration, while a vertical plane has the opposite limits.

angles = np.linspace(0,90,361)
radians = np.deg2rad(angles)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5, 4.4))
ax.plot(angles, np.sin(radians), color=BLACK, linewidth=2.5,
        label=r"downhill acceleration $|a|/g=\sin\alpha$")
ax.plot(angles, np.cos(radians), color=DARK_GOLD, linewidth=2.5,
        label=r"normal force $N/(mg)=\cos\alpha$")
ax.fill_between(angles, np.sin(radians), np.cos(radians), color=GOLD, alpha=0.18)
ax.set(xlabel="incline angle α (degrees)", ylabel="dimensionless magnitude",
       xlim=(0,90), ylim=(0,1.05), title="Changing angle redistributes gravity's components")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
plt.show()

Motion along the constraint for several angles¶

With the same initial position and velocity, steeper inclines produce faster downhill motion. Every trajectory still remains in the one-dimensional allowed coordinate sss.

time = np.linspace(0,2.2,300)
g = 9.81
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9.7, 4.1))
for angle, color in zip([10,25,40,60], [GRAY,GOLD,DARK_GOLD,BLACK]):
    radians = np.deg2rad(angle)
    s = -0.5*g*np.sin(radians)*time**2
    axes[0].plot(time, s, color=color, linewidth=2, label=f"α = {angle}°")
    axes[1].plot(s*np.cos(radians), s*np.sin(radians), color=color, linewidth=2)
axes[0].set(xlabel="time (s)", ylabel="along-slope coordinate s (m)",
            title="one-dimensional motion")
axes[0].legend(frameon=False)
axes[1].set(aspect="equal", xlabel="$x$ (m)", ylabel="$y$ (m)",
            title="the same motion in the xy-plane")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

ChatGPT-assisted extensions¶

For any of these assignments, students may use ChatGPT as a provisional collaborator, but the submission must include the important prompts, an independent mathematical check, at least one correction or limitation, and the student’s own conceptual explanation. ChatGPT output by itself is not evidence.

  1. Audit a constrained-motion derivation. Ask ChatGPT to derive the motion of a bead on a circle, ellipse, or moving wire using a Lagrange multiplier. Check every sign and derivative, compare with a reduced-coordinate or Newtonian derivation, and test energy conservation numerically. Interpret the multiplier as a physical constraint force.

  2. Design your own constraint. Choose a smooth curve or surface, use ChatGPT to help formulate ϕ(q)=0\phi(\mathbf q)=0ϕ(q)=0, and simulate a particle under a simple potential. Demonstrate numerically that the trajectory stays on the constraint, then explain how ∇ϕ\nabla\phi∇ϕ determines the force direction and where the method could fail.

  3. The multiplier is not itself the force. Replace a constraint ϕ=0\phi=0ϕ=0 by cϕ=0c\phi=0cϕ=0 or by another function with the same zero set. Ask ChatGPT what should happen to λ\lambdaλ, solve both formulations, and show that λ\lambdaλ changes while λ∇ϕ\lambda\nabla\phiλ∇ϕ does not. Explain why the physical force must be invariant under a relabeling of the constraint.
