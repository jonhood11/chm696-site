Learning goals¶

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • classify an ODE by order, linearity, and homogeneity;

  • explain the role of a fundamental set of homogeneous solutions;

  • use a Wronskian with its required hypotheses; and

  • solve a first-order linear ODE with an integrating factor.

Linear differential equations¶

  • A linear differential equation is an equation involving an unknown function y(x)y(x)y(x) and its derivatives, where each term acts as a linear operator. For example, a time derivative ddt\frac{d}{dt}dtd​ or multiplying by a function a(x)a(x)a(x) is a linear operator.

  • The general form of an nnn-th order linear differential equation is:

a0(x)y+a1(x)y′+a2(x)y′′+a3(x)y′′′+⋯+an(x)y(n)=b(x)a_0(x) y + a_1(x) y' + a_2(x) y'' + a_3(x) y''' + \dots + a_n(x) y^{(n)} = b(x)a0​(x)y+a1​(x)y′+a2​(x)y′′+a3​(x)y′′′+⋯+an​(x)y(n)=b(x)
(1)

  • yyy is the unknown function of xxx

  • y′,y′′,y(n)y', y'', y^{(n)}y′,y′′,y(n) denote the first, second, and nnn-th derivatives of yyy with respect to xxx.

  • The coefficients a0(x),a1(x),…,an(x)a_0(x), a_1(x), \dots, a_n(x)a0​(x),a1​(x),…,an​(x) may either be constants or functions of xxx.

  • The term b(x)b(x)b(x) is called the inhomogeneous term, but in physical systems, this is typically referred to as the drive or source term. For example, in a driven damped harmonic oscillator, this term represents the external driving force acting on the system.

The order of the differential equation¶

  • The order of the differential equation refers to the highest derivative present in the equation.

  • On an interval where the normalized coefficients are continuous and the leading coefficient does not vanish, the homogeneous solution space of an nnnth-order linear ODE has dimension nnn. An initial-value problem therefore requires nnn independent initial data.

  • These constants are determined by applying initial or boundary conditions relevant to the physical problem being modeled.

  • Example: A second-order harmonic oscillator has the general solution y(x)=Aeikx+Be−ikxy(x) = A e^{ikx} + B e^{-ikx}y(x)=Aeikx+Be−ikx, which contains two constants AAA and BBB, determined by the initial conditions.

  • Example: A first-order exponential decay equation, y(x)=Ae−κxy(x) = A e^{-\kappa x}y(x)=Ae−κx, has one constant, AAA, determined by a single initial condition.

Homogeneous and inhomogeneous equations¶

  • When b(x)=0b(x) = 0b(x)=0, the equation is termed homogeneous; otherwise, it is inhomogeneous.

  • In a homogeneous linear differential equation, the drive term is set to zero:

a0(x)y+a1(x)y′+a2(x)y′′+⋯+an(x)y(n)=0a_0(x) y + a_1(x) y' + a_2(x) y'' + \dots + a_n(x) y^{(n)} = 0a0​(x)y+a1​(x)y′+a2​(x)y′′+⋯+an​(x)y(n)=0
(2)

  • The solution to a homogeneous equation is particularly important because it forms the foundation for solving inhomogeneous equations.

  • Solutions to homogeneous equations are called homogeneous solutions, denoted as yh(x)y_h(x)yh​(x).

  • An inhomogeneous equation includes a non-zero drive term b(x)b(x)b(x), which represents external forces or inputs to the system.

  • A general solution of the inhomogeneous equation, y(x)y(x)y(x), can be written as the sum of the homogeneous solution and a particular solution, yp(x)y_p(x)yp​(x), that satisfies the inhomogeneous equation:

y(x)=yh(x)+yp(x)y(x) = y_h(x) + y_p(x)y(x)=yh​(x)+yp​(x)
(3)

Linear Dependence¶

  • A set of functions y1(x),y2(x),…,yn(x)y_1(x), y_2(x), \dots, y_n(x)y1​(x),y2​(x),…,yn​(x) is said to be linearly independent if no function in the set can be written as a linear combination of the others.

  • In other words, the only solution to the equation

c1y1(x)+c2y2(x)+⋯+cnyn(x)=0c_1 y_1(x) + c_2 y_2(x) + \dots + c_n y_n(x) = 0c1​y1​(x)+c2​y2​(x)+⋯+cn​yn​(x)=0
(4)

for all xxx is c1=c2=⋯=cn=0c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_n = 0c1​=c2​=⋯=cn​=0.

  • If the functions are not linearly independent, they are called linearly dependent.

  • Example of linearly independent functions: sin⁡(x)\sin(x)sin(x) and cos⁡(x)\cos(x)cos(x). The only solution to c1sin⁡(x)+c2cos⁡(x)=0c_1 \sin(x) + c_2 \cos(x) = 0c1​sin(x)+c2​cos(x)=0 for all xxx is c1=c2=0c_1 = c_2 = 0c1​=c2​=0.

  • Example of linearly dependent functions: exe^xex and 2ex2e^x2ex. In this case, 2ex2e^x2ex can be written as a constant multiple of exe^xex, so the two functions are linearly dependent.

The Wronskian¶

  • The Wronskian of two functions y1(x)y_1(x)y1​(x) and y2(x)y_2(x)y2​(x) is defined as:

W(y1,y2)=y1y2′−y2y1′W(y_1, y_2) = y_1y_2' - y_2y_1'W(y1​,y2​)=y1​y2′​−y2​y1′​
(5)

  • More generally, for nnn functions, the Wronskian is the determinant:

W(y1,…,yn)=∣y1y2⋯yny1′y2′⋯yn′⋮⋮⋱⋮y1(n−1)y2(n−1)⋯yn(n−1)∣W(y_1, \ldots, y_n) = \begin{vmatrix} y_1 & y_2 & \cdots & y_n \\ y_1' & y_2' & \cdots & y_n' \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ y_1^{(n-1)} & y_2^{(n-1)} & \cdots & y_n^{(n-1)} \end{vmatrix}W(y1​,…,yn​)=∣∣​y1​y1′​⋮y1(n−1)​​y2​y2′​⋮y2(n−1)​​⋯⋯⋱⋯​yn​yn′​⋮yn(n−1)​​∣∣​
(6)

  • If the Wronskian is nonzero at any point, the functions are linearly independent. The converse is not true for arbitrary differentiable functions: an identically zero Wronskian need not imply dependence. For solutions of the same linear homogeneous ODE with continuous coefficients, Abel’s identity supplies the stronger conclusion used in ODE theory.

  • Two functions f1(x)f_1(x)f1​(x) and f2(x)f_2(x)f2​(x) are linearly dependent if there exists a constant ccc such that f1(x)=c⋅f2(x)f_1(x) = c \cdot f_2(x)f1​(x)=c⋅f2​(x) for all xxx. Consequently, their derivatives are related by f1′(x)=c⋅f2′(x)f_1'(x) = c \cdot f_2'(x)f1′​(x)=c⋅f2′​(x). The Wronskian captures both these relationships in a single expression.

Linear First-Order Ordinary Differential Equations¶

  • The most general first-order linear ODE:

dydx+P(x)y=Q(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)dxdy​+P(x)y=Q(x)
(8)

  • Here, yyy is the unknown function, P(x)P(x)P(x) and Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) are functions of xxx but not of yyy. Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) is the drive term.

  • Note that in general we could also multiply y′y'y′ by a new function of xxx, but we could divide through by that function and absorb it into P(x)P(x)P(x) and Q(x)Q(x)Q(x).

  • We will be looking for a solution in the form of

y(x)=Cyh(x)+yp(x)y(x) = C y_h(x) + y_p(x)y(x)=Cyh​(x)+yp​(x)
(9)

  • CCC is an arbitrary constant determined by the initial conditions

  • yh(x)y_h(x)yh​(x) is the homogeneous solution that does not depend on the drive

  • yp(x)y_p(x)yp​(x) is the particular solution to the inhomogeneous equation with drive

Homogeneous Solution¶

  • First, let’s solve the homogeneous equation. In the case where Q(x)=0Q(x) = 0Q(x)=0, the equation simplifies to:

dydx=−P(x)y\frac{dy}{dx} = -P(x) ydxdy​=−P(x)y
(10)

  • We can solve this by separation of variables:

dyy=−P(x)dx\frac{dy}{y} = -P(x) dxydy​=−P(x)dx
(11)

  • Integrating both sides gives:

ln⁡∣y∣=−∫P(x) dx+C\ln|y| = - \int P(x) \, dx + Cln∣y∣=−∫P(x)dx+C
(12)

  • Exponentiating both sides yields the general solution:

y(x)=Ae−∫P(x)dxy(x) = A e^{-\int P(x)dx}y(x)=Ae−∫P(x)dx
(13)

where A=eCA = e^CA=eC is an arbitrary constant.

Inhomogeneous Equation¶

  • Now, let’s solve the inhomogeneous equation where Q(x)≠0Q(x) \neq 0Q(x)=0:
