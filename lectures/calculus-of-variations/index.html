Learning goals¶

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • derive the Euler--Lagrange equation from a first variation;

  • use cyclic-coordinate and Beltrami first integrals;

  • distinguish stationarity from minimization; and

  • connect variational mechanics with constraints and Noether symmetries.

Variational calculus¶

  • Variational calculus is a branch of mathematical analysis that deals with finding extrema (minimum or maximum values) of functionals, which are mappings from a set of functions to the real numbers. Unlike standard calculus, which focuses on functions of real variables, variational calculus focuses on functionals that depend on functions and their derivatives.

  • A functional is a rule that assigns a real number to a function. A common form of a functional is:

J[y]=∫abL(x,y(x),y′(x)) dxJ[y] = \int_{a}^{b} L(x, y(x), y'(x)) \, dxJ[y]=∫ab​L(x,y(x),y′(x))dx
(1)

where:

  • L(x,y,y′)L(x, y, y')L(x,y,y′) is called the Lagrangian and is a given function of xxx, y(x)y(x)y(x), and y′(x)y'(x)y′(x).

  • y(x)y(x)y(x) is the function to be determined, belonging to a suitable class of functions satisfying given boundary conditions.

  • y′(x)=dydxy'(x) = \frac{dy}{dx}y′(x)=dxdy​ is the derivative of y(x)y(x)y(x) with respect to xxx.

The Variational Problem¶

Functional:

J[y]=∫abL(x,y(x),y′(x)) dxJ[y] = \int_{a}^{b} L(x, y(x), y'(x)) \, dxJ[y]=∫ab​L(x,y(x),y′(x))dx
(2)

The fundamental problem is to find a function y(x)y(x)y(x) that makes J[y]J[y]J[y] stationary. Stationarity is a necessary condition for a smooth local extremum, but a stationary function may instead be a maximum or saddle point. Writing yϵ=y+ϵηy_\epsilon=y+\epsilon\etayϵ​=y+ϵη,

δJ[y;η]=ddϵJ[y+ϵη]∣ϵ=0=0\delta J[y;\eta] =\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}J[y+\epsilon\eta]\right|_{\epsilon=0} =0δJ[y;η]=dϵd​J[y+ϵη]∣∣​ϵ=0​=0
(3)

The Euler-Lagrange Equation¶

To find the function y(x)y(x)y(x) that makes J[y]J[y]J[y] stationary, we consider a small variation δy(x)\delta y(x)δy(x) that vanishes at the endpoints (δy(a)=δy(b)=0\delta y(a) = \delta y(b) = 0δy(a)=δy(b)=0) and compute the first variation δJ\delta JδJ:

δJ=∫ab[∂L∂yδy+∂L∂y′δy′]dx\delta J = \int_{a}^{b} \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} \delta y + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \delta y' \right] dxδJ=∫ab​[∂y∂L​δy+∂y′∂L​δy′]dx
(4)

Using integration by parts on the second term:

∫ab∂L∂y′δy′ dx=[∂L∂y′δy]ab−∫abddx(∂L∂y′)δy dx\int_{a}^{b} \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \delta y' \, dx = \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \delta y \right]_a^b - \int_{a}^{b} \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) \delta y \, dx∫ab​∂y′∂L​δy′dx=[∂y′∂L​δy]ab​−∫ab​dxd​(∂y′∂L​)δydx
(5)

Since δy(a)=δy(b)=0\delta y(a) = \delta y(b) = 0δy(a)=δy(b)=0, the boundary term vanishes, and we have:

δJ=∫ab(∂L∂y−ddx(∂L∂y′))δy dx\delta J = \int_{a}^{b} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) \right) \delta y \, dxδJ=∫ab​(∂y∂L​−dxd​(∂y′∂L​))δydx
(6)

For δJ=0\delta J = 0δJ=0 to hold for arbitrary δy\delta yδy, the integrand must be zero:

∂L∂y−ddx(∂L∂y′)=0\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) = 0∂y∂L​−dxd​(∂y′∂L​)=0
(7)

The Euler-Lagrange Equation (continued)¶

For the functional:

J[y]=∫abL(x,y(x),y′(x)) dxJ[y] = \int_{a}^{b} L(x, y(x), y'(x)) \, dxJ[y]=∫ab​L(x,y(x),y′(x))dx
(8)

This is the Euler-Lagrange equation is:

∂L∂y−ddx(∂L∂y′)=0\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) = 0∂y∂L​−dxd​(∂y′∂L​)=0
(9)

The Euler-Lagrange equation provides a necessary condition for y(x)y(x)y(x) to be an extremal function for J[y]J[y]J[y].

First Integrals of the Euler-Lagrange Equation¶

  • If LLL does not depend explicitly on yyy:

∂L∂y′=constant\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = \text{constant}∂y′∂L​=constant
(10)

  • If LLL does not depend explicitly on xxx, the Beltrami identity gives

L−y′∂L∂y′=constant.L-y'\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}=\text{constant}.L−y′∂y′∂L​=constant.
(11)

This follows by differentiating the left-hand side and using the Euler--Lagrange equation.

Generalizations¶

The Euler-Lagrange equation can be extended to functionals involving higher-order derivatives or multiple functions. For example, for functionals of the form:

J[y]=∫abL(x,y,y′,y′′) dxJ[y] = \int_{a}^{b} L(x, y, y', y'') \, dxJ[y]=∫ab​L(x,y,y′,y′′)dx
(29)

the Euler-Lagrange equation becomes:

∂L∂y−ddx(∂L∂y′)+d2dx2(∂L∂y′′)=0\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} \right) + \frac{d^2}{dx^2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial y''} \right) = 0∂y∂L​−dxd​(∂y′∂L​)+dx2d2​(∂y′′∂L​)=0
(30)

Variational Principle in Classical Mechanics¶

Classical mechanics can be formulated through:

  • Newton’s laws (forces),

  • Lagrangian mechanics (energy),

  • Variational principle: the physical path makes the action stationary.

The variational formulation is elegant and generalizes naturally to fields, quantum mechanics, and relativity.

Action Functional¶

Define the Lagrangian

L(q,q˙,t)=T−V,L(q, \dot q, t) = T - V,L(q,q˙​,t)=T−V,
(31)

where TTT is kinetic and VVV is potential energy.

Define the action:

S[q]=∫t1t2L(q,q˙,t) dt.S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q, \dot q, t) \, dt.S[q]=∫t1​t2​​L(q,q˙​,t)dt.
(32)

The true path taken by the system satisfies the principle of stationary action:

δS=0.\delta S = 0.δS=0.
(33)

Euler--Lagrange Equation¶

Apply a variation:

q(t)→q(t)+ϵ η(t),η(t1)=η(t2)=0.q(t) \rightarrow q(t) + \epsilon \, \eta(t), \quad \eta(t_1)=\eta(t_2)=0.q(t)→q(t)+ϵη(t),η(t1​)=η(t2​)=0.
(34)

Require:

ddϵS[q+ϵη]∣ϵ=0=0.\frac{d}{d\epsilon} S[q + \epsilon \eta] \bigg|_{\epsilon=0} = 0.dϵd​S[q+ϵη]∣∣​ϵ=0​=0.
(35)

This leads to the Euler--Lagrange equation:

ddt(∂L∂q˙)−∂L∂q=0.\frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0.dtd​(∂q˙​∂L​)−∂q∂L​=0.
(36)

This equation replaces Newton’s laws.

Example: Free Particle¶

For a particle of mass mmm:

L=T=12mq˙2.L = T = \tfrac{1}{2} m \dot q^2.L=T=21​mq˙​2.
(37)

Compute derivatives:

∂L∂q=0,∂L∂q˙=mq˙.\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} &= 0, \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} &= m \dot q. \end{aligned}∂q∂L​∂q˙​∂L​​=0,=mq˙​.​
(38)

Then Euler--Lagrange gives:

ddt(mq˙)=0  ⇒  mq¨=0.\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot q)=0 \; \Rightarrow \; m\ddot q = 0.dtd​(mq˙​)=0⇒mq¨​=0.
(39)

The particle moves with constant velocity.

Example: Harmonic Oscillator¶

Lagrangian:

L=12mq˙2−12kq2.L = \tfrac{1}{2} m \dot q^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} k q^2.L=21​mq˙​2−21​kq2.
(40)

Then

∂L∂q=−kq,∂L∂q˙=mq˙.\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} &= -k q, \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} &= m \dot q. \end{aligned}∂q∂L​∂q˙​∂L​​=−kq,=mq˙​.​

mq¨+kq=0.m \ddot q + k q = 0.mq¨​+kq=0.

We recover the familiar oscillator equation.

Generalized Coordinates¶

Lagrangian mechanics allows the use of any convenient coordinates:

  • polar coordinates,

  • angles in pendulums,

  • coordinates adapted to constraints.

Example: simple pendulum (angle θ\thetaθ):

L=12mℓ2θ˙2−mgℓ(1−cos⁡θ).L = \tfrac{1}{2} m \ell^2 \dot \theta^2 - mg\ell(1 - \cos \theta).L=21​mℓ2θ˙2−mgℓ(1−cosθ).

Euler--Lagrange equation:

θ¨+gℓsin⁡θ=0.\ddot\theta + \frac{g}{\ell} \sin \theta = 0.θ¨+ℓg​sinθ=0.
(44)

Advantages of the Variational Method¶

  • Coordinates chosen freely.

  • Constraints handled systematically.

  • Natural extension to fields and quantum mechanics.

  • Symmetries lead directly to conserved quantities (Noether’s theorem).

Noether’s Theorem¶

Noether’s Theorem: Every continuous symmetry of the action corresponds to a conserved quantity.

If the action is invariant under a transformation

q→q+ϵ δq,q \rightarrow q + \epsilon \, \delta q,q→q+ϵδq,
(45)

then there exists a conserved current.

Constraints in Lagrangian Mechanics¶

Many mechanical systems have constraints, e.g.

  • a particle constrained to move on a surface,

  • fixed-length pendulum (constraint: x2+y2=ℓ2x^2 + y^2 = \ell^2x2+y2=ℓ2),

  • rolling without slipping.

A constraint can be written as

f(q1,q2,…,t)=0.f(q_1, q_2, \dots, t) = 0.f(q1​,q2​,…,t)=0.
(51)

To enforce the constraint, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier λ(t)\lambda(t)λ(t).

Lagrangian With a Constraint¶

Given an unconstrained Lagrangian L(q,q˙)L(q, \dot q)L(q,q˙​) and a constraint f(q,t)=0f(q,t)=0f(q,t)=0, the modified Lagrangian is

L′=L+λ(t)f(q,t).L' = L + \lambda(t) f(q,t).L′=L+λ(t)f(q,t).
(52)

Euler--Lagrange equations become

ddt(∂L′∂q˙i)−∂L′∂qi=0.\frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L'}{\partial \dot q_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L'}{\partial q_i} = 0.dtd​(∂q˙​i​∂L′​)−∂qi​∂L′​=0.
(53)

The variation with respect to λ\lambdaλ enforces the constraint:

∂L′∂λ=f(q,t)=0.\frac{\partial L'}{\partial \lambda} = f(q,t) = 0.∂λ∂L′​=f(q,t)=0.
(54)

Example: Particle on a Circle¶

Constraint: particle moves on a circle of radius RRR:

f(x,y)=x2+y2−R2=0.f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - R^2 = 0.f(x,y)=x2+y2−R2=0.
(55)

Kinetic energy:

T=12m(x˙2+y˙2).T = \tfrac{1}{2} m (\dot x^2 + \dot y^2).T=21​m(x˙2+y˙​2).
(56)

Constrained Lagrangian:

L′=12m(x˙2+y˙2)+λ(x2+y2−R2).L' = \tfrac{1}{2} m (\dot x^2 + \dot y^2) + \lambda(x^2 + y^2 - R^2).L′=21​m(x˙2+y˙​2)+λ(x2+y2−R2).
(57)

Variation yields equations of motion and the constraint force term (from λ\lambdaλ).

Interpretation of the Lagrange Multiplier¶

The multiplier determines the generalized constraint force through Qi(c)=λ ∂f/∂qiQ_i^{(c)}=\lambda\,\partial f/\partial q_iQi(c)​=λ∂f/∂qi​ for this sign convention.

Example: for a bead constrained to a wire:

  • The constraint force keeps the particle on the wire.

  • It does no virtual work along allowed displacements.

  • Its physical magnitude follows only after combining λ\lambdaλ with the gradient and normalization of the constraint.

Thus λ\lambdaλ is not arbitrary, but it is not itself a normalization- independent force magnitude; rescaling fff rescales λ\lambdaλ.
