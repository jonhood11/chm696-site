Normal Modes

Purdue University

Before we calculate anything, play with the masses and springs below. Can you find a motion in which all the masses oscillate at the same frequency and keep a fixed pattern of relative amplitudes? That pattern is a normal mode. Mixing modes gives more complicated motion.

Now let’s derive the two modes of a simple system with two masses.

Coupled oscillators¶

Let’s start with two equal masses mmm connected by three identical springs, each with spring constant KKK. The outer ends are attached to fixed walls. The masses move horizontally without friction, and we neglect damping.

If we move one mass, the middle spring also exerts a force on the other mass. The two motions are coupled. We want to find the patterns in which both masses oscillate at a single frequency.

For the left mass, Newton’s second law gives

F=mx¨=md2xdt2F = m \ddot{x} = m \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}F=mx¨=mdt2d2x​
(1)

Here xxx and yyy are displacements from equilibrium, not the distance between the masses. The left spring contributes −Kx-Kx−Kx. The middle spring’s change in length is y−xy-xy−x, so its force on the left mass is K(y−x)K(y-x)K(y−x). Adding the forces,

mx¨=−Kx+K(y−x)=−2Kx+Kym \ddot{x} = -Kx + K(y - x) = -2Kx + Kymx¨=−Kx+K(y−x)=−2Kx+Ky
(2)

For the right mass, the outer spring contributes −Ky-Ky−Ky, and the middle spring exerts the equal and opposite force K(x−y)K(x-y)K(x−y):

my¨=−Ky+K(x−y)=Kx−2Kym \ddot{y} = -Ky + K(x - y) = Kx - 2Kymy¨​=−Ky+K(x−y)=Kx−2Ky
(3)

Assuming Oscillatory Motion¶

To find the normal modes, assume both xxx and yyy undergo simple harmonic oscillations:

x=x0eiωt,y=y0eiωtx = x_0 e^{i \omega t}, \quad y = y_0 e^{i \omega t}x=x0​eiωt,y=y0​eiωt
(4)

Substituting these into the equations of motion, we get:

−mω2x0eiωt=−2Kx0eiωt+Ky0eiωt-m \omega^2 x_0 e^{i \omega t} = -2Kx_0 e^{i \omega t} + Ky_0 e^{i \omega t}−mω2x0​eiωt=−2Kx0​eiωt+Ky0​eiωt
(5)

Dividing through by eiωte^{i \omega t}eiωt, we obtain:

−mω2x0=−2Kx0+Ky0-m \omega^2 x_0 = -2Kx_0 + Ky_0−mω2x0​=−2Kx0​+Ky0​
(6)

Similarly for yyy, we have:

−mω2y0=Kx0−2Ky0-m \omega^2 y_0 = Kx_0 - 2Ky_0−mω2y0​=Kx0​−2Ky0​
(7)

This can be written in matrix form as:

[−mω2+2K−K−K−mω2+2K][x0y0]=0\begin{bmatrix} -m \omega^2 + 2K & -K \\ -K & -m \omega^2 + 2K \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{bmatrix} = 0[−mω2+2K−K​−K−mω2+2K​][x0​y0​​]=0
(8)

Dividing by KKK, we introduce a dimensionless parameter λ=mω2K\lambda = \frac{m \omega^2}{K}λ=Kmω2​, giving:

λ[x0y0]=[2−1−12][x0y0]\lambda \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{bmatrix}λ[x0​y0​​]=[2−1​−12​][x0​y0​​]
(9)

This is an eigenvalue problem:

Av=λv,v=[x0y0]A \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}, \quad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{bmatrix}Av=λv,v=[x0​y0​​]
(10)

where AAA is a real and symmetric matrix:

A=[2−1−12]A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}A=[2−1​−12​]
(11)

Here the two off-diagonal entries are equal: the coupling between the masses appears symmetrically in the two equations. Thus AT=AA^T=AAT=A. This symmetry gives us a useful property of the mode vectors.

Orthogonality of the mode vectors¶

Theorem 1 (Eigenvectors of a real symmetric matrix)

For a real symmetric matrix AT=AA^T=AAT=A, real eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal and therefore linearly independent.

Proof 1

Let Avi=λiviA\mathbf{v}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{v}_iAvi​=λi​vi​ and Avj=λjvjA\mathbf{v}_j=\lambda_j\mathbf{v}_jAvj​=λj​vj​, with λi≠λj\lambda_i\ne\lambda_jλi​=λj​. Using the second eigenvalue equation gives

viTAvj=λjviTvj.\mathbf{v}_i^T A\mathbf{v}_j =\lambda_j\mathbf{v}_i^T\mathbf{v}_j.viT​Avj​=λj​viT​vj​.
(12)

But symmetry lets us move AAA to the other vector:

viTAvj=(Avi)Tvj=λiviTvj.\mathbf{v}_i^T A\mathbf{v}_j =(A\mathbf{v}_i)^T\mathbf{v}_j =\lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i^T\mathbf{v}_j.viT​Avj​=(Avi​)Tvj​=λi​viT​vj​.
(13)

Subtracting these expressions,

(λi−λj)viTvj=0.(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\mathbf{v}_i^T\mathbf{v}_j=0.(λi​−λj​)viT​vj​=0.
(14)

Since the eigenvalues differ, viTvj=0\mathbf{v}_i^T\mathbf{v}_j=0viT​vj​=0. The nonzero eigenvectors are orthogonal, so they are also linearly independent.

For our equal-mass system, this means modes with different squared frequencies have perpendicular displacement vectors. With unequal masses, the corresponding orthogonality uses the mass-weighted inner product instead.

Real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and admit real eigenvectors; we will return to that result in the later discussion of complex spaces. The proof above uses only real vectors and the transpose.

Eigenmodes¶

Solving the eigenvalue problem yields two eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors.

Eigenvalue 1¶

The first eigenvalue and eigenvector are:

λ1=1,v1=[11]\lambda_1 = 1, \quad \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}λ1​=1,v1​=[11​]
(15)

The corresponding angular frequency is:

ω1=Km\omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{K}{m}}ω1​=mK​​
(16)

The real part of the oscillatory solution for this mode is:

ℜ[x(t)y(t)]=[cos⁡(ω1t)cos⁡(ω1t)]\Re \begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ y(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\omega_1 t) \\ \cos(\omega_1 t) \end{bmatrix}ℜ[x(t)y(t)​]=[cos(ω1​t)cos(ω1​t)​]
(17)

Eigenvalue 2¶

The second eigenvalue and eigenvector are:

λ2=3,v2=[1−1]\lambda_2 = 3, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}λ2​=3,v2​=[1−1​]
(18)

The corresponding angular frequency is:

ω2=3Km\omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{3K}{m}}ω2​=m3K​​
(19)

The real part of the oscillatory solution for this mode is:

ℜ[x(t)y(t)]=[cos⁡(ω2t)−cos⁡(ω2t)]\Re\begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ y(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\omega_2 t) \\ -\cos(\omega_2 t) \end{bmatrix}ℜ[x(t)y(t)​]=[cos(ω2​t)−cos(ω2​t)​]
(20)

The total motion¶

The masses do not have to move in just one normal mode. Because the equations of motion are linear, a sum of mode solutions is also a solution. Our two eigenvectors span the displacement space, so together they describe any motion of this system.

Writing x(t)=(x(t),y(t))T\mathbf{x}(t)=(x(t),y(t))^Tx(t)=(x(t),y(t))T, the general solution is

x(t)=∑j=12[Ajcos⁡(ωjt)+Bjsin⁡(ωjt)]vj.\mathbf{x}(t)= \sum_{j=1}^{2} \left[A_j\cos(\omega_jt)+B_j\sin(\omega_jt)\right]\mathbf{v}_j.x(t)=j=1∑2​[Aj​cos(ωj​t)+Bj​sin(ωj​t)]vj​.
(21)

Each mode oscillates independently at its own frequency. The initial positions and velocities determine its coefficients:

x(0)=∑j=12Ajvj,x˙(0)=∑j=12ωjBjvj.\mathbf{x}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}A_j\mathbf{v}_j, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{x}}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\omega_j B_j\mathbf{v}_j.x(0)=j=1∑2​Aj​vj​,x˙(0)=j=1∑2​ωj​Bj​vj​.
(22)

If both masses are released from rest, the BjB_jBj​ vanish. Using the eigenvectors we just found,

(x(t)y(t))=A1(11)cos⁡(ω1t)+A2(1−1)cos⁡(ω2t).\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\y(t)\end{pmatrix} = A_1\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\cos(\omega_1t) + A_2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\cos(\omega_2t).(x(t)y(t)​)=A1​(11​)cos(ω1​t)+A2​(1−1​)cos(ω2​t).
(23)

Thus each mass’s displacement contains contributions from both modes. The two amplitude sliders below control A1A_1A1​ and A2A_2A2​; the simulation starts with zero initial velocities.

For a system with more masses and positive normal-mode frequencies, the same expression applies with a sum over all its modes.

Seeing the normal modes¶

We can now compare the two patterns of motion. In the simulation, x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​ denote the displacements we called xxx and yyy above. Start with both masses and all three spring constants set to 1.

In Mode 1, the masses move together, following the eigenvector (1,1)T(1,1)^T(1,1)T. In Mode 2, they move in opposite directions, following (1,−1)T(1,-1)^T(1,−1)T, at a frequency 3\sqrt{3}3​ times larger. The amplitudes control how much of each mode is present, not the amplitude of each mass separately.

The left panel shows the physical motion. The right panel plots the pair of displacements as one vector (x1,x2)(x_1,x_2)(x1​,x2​). In a single mode, its tip moves back and forth along the corresponding dashed eigenvector line. With both modes present, the two contributions add and the tip follows a more complicated path.
