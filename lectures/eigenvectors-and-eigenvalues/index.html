Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

Purdue University

Last time we looked at how a matrix transforms vectors. Usually it changes both their length and their direction. Today we will ask whether a matrix has preferred directions along which its action becomes especially simple. These will turn out to be its eigenvector directions. If we find enough independent eigenvector directions, they form a basis.

Let’s start by looking at how a matrix transforms a vector on the unit circle.

Open in new tab ↗

The “Symmetric stretch” example uses

M=(2112).\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.M=(21​12​).
(1)

Keep this matrix fixed and rotate the blue vector v\mathbf{v}v with the angle slider or by dragging its tip. The orange arrow is Mv\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}Mv. Are there directions where the two arrows lie on the same line, so the transformation only stretches or shrinks the vector? How do their lengths compare there?

For this matrix, those directions are the two diagonals, through (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1). Along the first, the vector is stretched by a factor of three; along the second, it is unchanged. These are eigenvector directions, and the scale factors 3 and 1 are the corresponding eigenvalues.

We can now write down the condition we have been looking for:

Mv=λv,v≠0.\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v}\ne\mathbf{0}.Mv=λv,v=0.
(2)

A nonzero vector satisfying this equation is an eigenvector of M\mathbf{M}M, and λ\lambdaλ is its eigenvalue. The vector need not be unchanged: a positive eigenvalue scales it along the same direction, a negative eigenvalue also reverses it, and a zero eigenvalue sends it to the origin.

For our example, the eigenvalues are 3 and 1. Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is another eigenvector with the same eigenvalue, so it is the direction that matters, not our choice of length.

Why are these directions useful? If we can expand a vector in a basis of eigenvectors, applying the matrix simply scales each component by its eigenvalue. We will use this to simplify calculations later in the lecture.

Now let’s find these directions and scale factors algebraically. For a general 2×22\times22×2 matrix, the eigenvalue equation is

[abcd]v=λv\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}[ac​bd​]v=λv
(3)

which becomes

[abcd]v=[λ00λ]v.\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}.[ac​bd​]v=[λ0​0λ​]v.
(4)

Then we can subtract the right-hand side to get

[a−λbcd−λ]v=0.\begin{bmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{v} = 0.[a−λc​bd−λ​]v=0.
(5)

We are looking for a nonzero vector that M−λI\mathbf M-\lambda\mathbf IM−λI sends to zero. This is possible only if the matrix is singular, so its determinant must vanish. We therefore obtain the characteristic equation

(a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=0,(a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = 0,(a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=0,
(6)

This quadratic has two roots, λ1\lambda_1λ1​ and λ2\lambda_2λ2​, counting repetitions. The two roots may be distinct, or they may be the same repeated root. They may also be real or complex.

More generally, for an N×NN\times NN×N matrix,

det⁡(M−λI)=0\det(\mathbf M-\lambda\mathbf I)=0det(M−λI)=0
(7)

is a polynomial of degree NNN. It therefore has NNN roots counting repetitions when complex roots are allowed, but it may have fewer than NNN distinct eigenvalues. For example, the identity matrix has just one distinct eigenvalue, 1, repeated NNN times.

Once we know a root λn\lambda_nλn​, we find its eigenvectors by solving

Mvn=λnvn,or equivalently(M−λnI)vn=0.\mathbf M\mathbf v_n=\lambda_n\mathbf v_n, \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad (\mathbf M-\lambda_n\mathbf I)\mathbf v_n=\mathbf 0.Mvn​=λn​vn​,or equivalently(M−λn​I)vn​=0.
(8)

Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is another eigenvector, so the solution is not unique. We can choose its length by normalizing it, usually so that ∣vn∣2=1|\mathbf v_n|^2=1∣vn​∣2=1. A repeated eigenvalue may have more than one independent eigenvector—or it may not. We examine this distinction next.

Do the eigenvectors form a basis?¶

For a 2×22\times22×2 matrix, we need two linearly independent eigenvectors to form a basis. Whether we get them depends on whether the two roots of the characteristic equation are distinct or repeated.

Theorem 1 (Distinct eigenvalues give independent eigenvectors)

If a 2×22\times22×2 matrix has two distinct eigenvalues λ1≠λ2\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2λ1​=λ2​, then their eigenvectors v1\mathbf v_1v1​ and v2\mathbf v_2v2​ are linearly independent. They are not necessarily orthogonal.

Proof 1

Suppose instead that the eigenvectors point along the same direction, so v2=cv1\mathbf v_2=c\mathbf v_1v2​=cv1​ for some c≠0c\ne0c=0. Then

Mv2=cMv1=cλ1v1=λ1v2.\mathbf M\mathbf v_2 =c\mathbf M\mathbf v_1 =c\lambda_1\mathbf v_1 =\lambda_1\mathbf v_2.Mv2​=cMv1​=cλ1​v1​=λ1​v2​.
(9)

But Mv2=λ2v2\mathbf M\mathbf v_2=\lambda_2\mathbf v_2Mv2​=λ2​v2​ as well, so (λ1−λ2)v2=0(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\mathbf v_2=\mathbf 0(λ1​−λ2​)v2​=0. Since v2≠0\mathbf v_2\ne\mathbf 0v2​=0, this would require λ1=λ2\lambda_1=\lambda_2λ1​=λ2​, contradicting our assumption.

Repeated eigenvalues. When an eigenvalue repeats, this theorem no longer gives us the answer. We have to check how many independent eigenvectors belong to that eigenvalue. Compare

A=(1001),B=(1101).\mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.A=(10​01​),B=(10​11​).
(10)

Both have characteristic polynomial (1−λ)2(1-\lambda)^2(1−λ)2, so both have the eigenvalue 1 twice.

For A\mathbf AA, every nonzero vector satisfies Av=v\mathbf A\mathbf v=\mathbf vAv=v. We can choose (1,0)T(1,0)^T(1,0)T and (0,1)T(0,1)^T(0,1)T as two independent eigenvectors.

For B\mathbf BB, solve the eigenvector equation:

(B−I)(xy)=(0100)(xy)=(y0)=(00).(\mathbf B-\mathbf I)\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}y\\0\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}.(B−I)(xy​)=(00​10​)(xy​)=(y0​)=(00​).
(11)

This requires y=0y=0y=0. Every eigenvector is a nonzero multiple of (1,0)T(1,0)^T(1,0)T, so there is only one independent eigenvector. Thus the same repeated root gives two independent eigenvectors for A\mathbf AA, but only one for B\mathbf BB.

The practical rule is simple: distinct eigenvalues guarantee independent eigenvectors; repeated eigenvalues must be checked by solving (M−λI)v=0(\mathbf M-\lambda\mathbf I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0(M−λI)v=0. We will see how an eigenvector basis diagonalizes a matrix in Similarity Transformations and Diagonalization.
