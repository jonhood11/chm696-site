Similarity Transformations and Diagonalization

Purdue University

Why use an eigenvector basis?¶

We have found the special directions of a matrix. Along an eigenvector, the matrix does not mix directions: it only multiplies that vector by its eigenvalue. This suggests a natural question:

What does the matrix look like if we use its eigenvectors as our coordinate axes?

Suppose an n×nn\times nn×n matrix M\mathbf MM has nnn linearly independent eigenvectors u1,…,un\mathbf u_1,\ldots,\mathbf u_nu1​,…,un​. Place them in the columns of a matrix:

S=[ u1 u2 ⋯ un ].\mathbf S=[\,\mathbf u_1\ \mathbf u_2\ \cdots\ \mathbf u_n\,].S=[u1​ u2​ ⋯ un​].
(1)

The matrix S\mathbf SS converts eigenvector-basis coordinates into the original coordinates, while S−1\mathbf S^{-1}S−1 performs the reverse conversion:

eigenvector coordinates→ S original coordinates,original coordinates→ S−1 eigenvector coordinates.\boxed{\text{eigenvector coordinates}} \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf S\ } \boxed{\text{original coordinates}}, \qquad \boxed{\text{original coordinates}} \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf S^{-1}\ } \boxed{\text{eigenvector coordinates}}.eigenvector coordinates​ S ​original coordinates​,original coordinates​ S−1 ​eigenvector coordinates​.
(2)

For example, if c=(c1,…,cn)T\mathbf c=(c_1,\ldots,c_n)^Tc=(c1​,…,cn​)T contains the coordinates in the eigenvector basis, then

v=Sc=c1u1+⋯+cnun,c=S−1v.\mathbf v=\mathbf S\mathbf c =c_1\mathbf u_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf u_n, \qquad \mathbf c=\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf v.v=Sc=c1​u1​+⋯+cn​un​,c=S−1v.
(3)

In this basis, applying M\mathbf MM should simply multiply each coordinate by its eigenvalue.

Theorem 1 (Diagonalization in an eigenvector basis)

An n×nn\times nn×n matrix M\mathbf MM is diagonalizable if and only if it has nnn linearly independent eigenvectors. The eigenvectors must belong to the coordinate space we are using: real eigenvectors for a real change of basis, or complex eigenvectors if we allow complex coordinates.

Place those eigenvectors in the columns of S\mathbf SS:

S=[ u1 u2 ⋯ un ].\mathbf S=[\,\mathbf u_1\ \mathbf u_2\ \cdots\ \mathbf u_n\,].S=[u1​ u2​ ⋯ un​].
(4)

Then

S−1MS=D=diag⁡(λ1,λ2,…,λn).\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S =\mathbf D =\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n).S−1MS=D=diag(λ1​,λ2​,…,λn​).
(5)
Proof 1

Multiplying a matrix by S\mathbf SS applies it to each column. Since Muj=λjuj\mathbf M\mathbf u_j=\lambda_j\mathbf u_jMuj​=λj​uj​,

MS=[ Mu1 ⋯ Mun ]=[ λ1u1 ⋯ λnun ]=SD.\begin{aligned} \mathbf M\mathbf S &=[\,\mathbf M\mathbf u_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf M\mathbf u_n\,]\\ &=[\,\lambda_1\mathbf u_1\ \cdots\ \lambda_n\mathbf u_n\,]\\ &=\mathbf S\mathbf D. \end{aligned}MS​=[Mu1​ ⋯ Mun​]=[λ1​u1​ ⋯ λn​un​]=SD.​
(6)

The columns are linearly independent, so S\mathbf SS is invertible. Multiplying on the left by S−1\mathbf S^{-1}S−1 gives S−1MS=D\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S=\mathbf DS−1MS=D.

Conversely, if an invertible S\mathbf SS gives S−1MS=D\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S=\mathbf DS−1MS=D, then MS=SD\mathbf M\mathbf S=\mathbf S\mathbf DMS=SD. Reading this column by column shows that the columns of S\mathbf SS are eigenvectors. They are linearly independent because S\mathbf SS is invertible.

Read the diagonalization from right to left:

eigenvector coordinates→ S original coordinates→ M transformed vector→ S−1 new eigenvector coordinates.\boxed{\text{eigenvector coordinates}} \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf S\ } \boxed{\text{original coordinates}} \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf M\ } \boxed{\text{transformed vector}} \xrightarrow{\ \mathbf S^{-1}\ } \boxed{\text{new eigenvector coordinates}}.eigenvector coordinates​ S ​original coordinates​ M ​transformed vector​ S−1 ​new eigenvector coordinates​.
(7)

The final result is diagonal because each eigenvector component evolves independently:

c′=Dc=(λ1c1⋮λncn).\mathbf c'=\mathbf D\mathbf c =\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1c_1\\ \vdots\\ \lambda_nc_n \end{pmatrix}.c′=Dc=⎝⎛​λ1​c1​⋮λn​cn​​⎠⎞​.
(8)

Can we do this for every matrix? Not always. We need enough linearly independent eigenvectors to form a basis. Recall the two repeated-eigenvalue examples: the identity matrix had a full eigenvector basis, while the shear matrix had only one independent eigenvector. Repeated roots alone do not decide whether diagonalization is possible.

For a 2×22\times22×2 matrix with two distinct eigenvalues, the condition is automatically satisfied because the two eigenvectors are linearly independent. For an n×nn\times nn×n matrix, nnn distinct eigenvalues are sufficient. They are not necessary: a repeated eigenvalue may still have enough independent eigenvectors.

Worked example¶

Return to the symmetric matrix from the eigenvector lecture:

M=(2112).\mathbf M= \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.M=(21​12​).
(9)

Its eigenpairs are

λ1=3,u1=(11),λ2=1,u2=(1−1).\lambda_1=3, \quad \mathbf u_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_2=1, \quad \mathbf u_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.λ1​=3,u1​=(11​),λ2​=1,u2​=(1−1​).
(10)

Put the eigenvectors into the columns of S\mathbf SS:

S=(111−1),S−1=12(111−1).\mathbf S= \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf S^{-1}=\frac12 \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.S=(11​1−1​),S−1=21​(11​1−1​).
(11)

Then

S−1MS=12(111−1)(2112)(111−1)=(3001).\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S =\frac12 \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.S−1MS=21​(11​1−1​)(21​12​)(11​1−1​)=(30​01​).
(12)

The original matrix mixes the xxx and yyy coordinates. In the eigenvector basis, it independently multiplies the symmetric component by 3 and the antisymmetric component by 1.

This example is especially simple because M\mathbf MM is symmetric and its eigenvectors are orthogonal. In general, S\mathbf SS only needs to be invertible. Its columns need not be orthogonal, and we cannot usually replace S−1\mathbf S^{-1}S−1 by S†\mathbf S^\daggerS†.

General similarity transformations¶

Diagonalization is one particularly useful change of coordinates. We can describe any invertible change of coordinates in the same way.

Suppose M\mathbf MM takes an input vector to an output vector in the original coordinates:

v2=Mv1.\mathbf v_2=\mathbf M\mathbf v_1.v2​=Mv1​.
(13)

Let an invertible matrix R\mathbf RR convert the old coordinate columns into new ones:

v1′=Rv1,v2′=Rv2.\mathbf v_1'=\mathbf R\mathbf v_1, \qquad \mathbf v_2'=\mathbf R\mathbf v_2.v1′​=Rv1​,v2′​=Rv2​.
(14)

What matrix M′\mathbf M'M′ represents the same linear map in the new coordinates?

Theorem 2 (Similarity transformation)

If coordinates change according to v′=Rv\mathbf v'=\mathbf R\mathbf vv′=Rv, then the matrix representing the same linear transformation in the new coordinates is

M′=RMR−1.\mathbf M'=\mathbf R\mathbf M\mathbf R^{-1}.M′=RMR−1.
(15)

The matrices M\mathbf MM and M′\mathbf M'M′ are called similar.

Proof 2

Apply R\mathbf RR to the original output and use v1=R−1v1′\mathbf v_1=\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf v_1'v1​=R−1v1′​:

v2′=Rv2=RMv1=RMR−1v1′.\mathbf v_2' =\mathbf R\mathbf v_2 =\mathbf R\mathbf M\mathbf v_1 =\mathbf R\mathbf M\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf v_1'.v2′​=Rv2​=RMv1​=RMR−1v1′​.
(16)

Because this holds for every input v1′\mathbf v_1'v1′​, the new matrix is M′=RMR−1\mathbf M'=\mathbf R\mathbf M\mathbf R^{-1}M′=RMR−1.

The linear map has not changed; only its matrix representation has. Read the product from right to left: return to the original coordinates with R−1\mathbf R^{-1}R−1, apply M\mathbf MM, and convert the result into the new coordinates with R\mathbf RR.

For diagonalization, the new coordinates are the eigenvector coefficients. Since S\mathbf SS maps eigenvector coordinates to original coordinates, the coordinate-change matrix in the theorem is

R=S−1.\mathbf R=\mathbf S^{-1}.R=S−1.
(17)

Therefore the general expression RMR−1\mathbf R\mathbf M\mathbf R^{-1}RMR−1 becomes

S−1MS=D.\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S=\mathbf D.S−1MS=D.
(18)

This is the precise connection between a general similarity transformation and diagonalization.

What diagonalization gives us¶

Powers of a matrix¶

A diagonal matrix is easy to raise to a nonnegative integer power:

Dp=(λ1p00λ2p).\mathbf D^p= \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1^p&0\\0&\lambda_2^p\end{pmatrix}.Dp=(λ1p​0​0λ2p​​).
(19)

Since M=SDS−1\mathbf M=\mathbf S\mathbf D\mathbf S^{-1}M=SDS−1, the adjacent factors S−1S\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf SS−1S cancel when we multiply copies of M\mathbf MM. Thus

Mp=SDpS−1.\mathbf M^p=\mathbf S\mathbf D^p\mathbf S^{-1}.Mp=SDpS−1.
(20)

We can calculate in the eigenvector basis and then transform back.

Trace and determinant¶

Two simple quantities provide useful checks on eigenvalue calculations. The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries,

tr⁡(M)=∑i=1nMii,\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf M)=\sum_{i=1}^n M_{ii},tr(M)=i=1∑n​Mii​,
(21)

and the determinant measures the total signed volume scaling of the transformation. Both are unchanged by a similarity transformation.

For the determinant,

det⁡(S−1MS)=det⁡(S−1)det⁡(M)det⁡(S)=det⁡(M).\det(\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S) =\det(\mathbf S^{-1})\det(\mathbf M)\det(\mathbf S) =\det(\mathbf M).det(S−1MS)=det(S−1)det(M)det(S)=det(M).
(22)

For the trace, use the cyclic identity

tr⁡(AB)=tr⁡(BA),\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf A\mathbf B) =\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf B\mathbf A),tr(AB)=tr(BA),
(23)

which follows directly from

tr⁡(AB)=∑i,jAijBji=∑j,iBjiAij.\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf A\mathbf B) =\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji} =\sum_{j,i}B_{ji}A_{ij}.tr(AB)=i,j∑​Aij​Bji​=j,i∑​Bji​Aij​.
(24)

Therefore,

tr⁡(S−1MS)=tr⁡(MSS−1)=tr⁡(M).\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf S^{-1}\mathbf M\mathbf S) =\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf M\mathbf S\mathbf S^{-1}) =\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf M).tr(S−1MS)=tr(MSS−1)=tr(M).
(25)

If M\mathbf MM is diagonalizable, we can evaluate these quantities in the eigenvector basis:

tr⁡(M)=∑j=1nλj,det⁡(M)=∏j=1nλj.\boxed{ \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf M)=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j, \qquad \det(\mathbf M)=\prod_{j=1}^n\lambda_j }.tr(M)=j=1∑n​λj​,det(M)=j=1∏n​λj​​.
(26)

Eigenvalues are counted with multiplicity. These two identities remain true even when a matrix is not diagonalizable; the general proof follows from the coefficients of its characteristic polynomial.

Checking normal-mode frequencies¶

For two equal masses and three equal springs, the equations of motion are x¨=−Ax\ddot{\mathbf x}=-\mathbf A\mathbf xx¨=−Ax, with

A=km(2−1−12).\mathbf A=\frac{k}{m} \begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.A=mk​(2−1​−12​).
(27)

Its eigenvalues are the squared angular frequencies. Before solving for either mode, the trace tells us

ω12+ω22=tr⁡(A)=4km.\omega_1^2+\omega_2^2=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf A)=\frac{4k}{m}.ω12​+ω22​=tr(A)=m4k​.
(28)

If we have found ω12=k/m\omega_1^2=k/mω12​=k/m, the other must be ω22=3k/m\omega_2^2=3k/mω22​=3k/m. The determinant provides a second check:

ω12ω22=det⁡(A)=3(km)2.\omega_1^2\omega_2^2=\det(\mathbf A)=3\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^2.ω12​ω22​=det(A)=3(mk​)2.
(29)

These identities are also useful for checking eigenvalues returned by a numerical calculation.
