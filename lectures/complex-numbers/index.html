From wave phase to the complex plane¶

Many physical systems oscillate. A simple real wave can be written as

E(t)=E0cos⁡(ωt+ϕ).E(t)=E_0\cos(\omega t+\phi).E(t)=E0​cos(ωt+ϕ).
(1)

The amplitude E0E_0E0​ tells us the size of the oscillation, while the phase ωt+ϕ\omega t+\phiωt+ϕ tells us where we are in the cycle. We can picture that phase as an arrow rotating around a circle. The horizontal projection of the arrow is the cosine wave that we measure.

This suggests a mathematical question:

Can we build a number that carries both an amplitude and a phase and that rotates naturally as time passes?

We want this object to support two operations. Addition should combine waves, because fields superpose. Multiplication should change the amplitude or advance the phase, because wave evolution moves the arrow around the circle.

An ordinary real number lives on a line. It can describe the horizontal projection of the arrow, but it cannot record where the complete arrow points. The natural state space is therefore a two-dimensional plane. An arrow in that plane has Cartesian coordinates

(x,y)=(rcos⁡θ,rsin⁡θ).(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta).(x,y)=(rcosθ,rsinθ).
(2)

Its length rrr is the amplitude, and its angle θ\thetaθ is the phase. At this stage it is simply a two-dimensional vector, and vectors already add in the right way for superposition:

(x1,y1)+(x2,y2)=(x1+x2,y1+y2).(x_1,y_1)+(x_2,y_2)=(x_1+x_2,y_1+y_2).(x1​,y1​)+(x2​,y2​)=(x1​+x2​,y1​+y2​).
(3)

We can package the two coordinates into one number. Use 1 for the horizontal basis direction and introduce a new symbol i\mathrm{i}i for the vertical basis direction. The arrow is then represented by the complex number

z=x+iy,z=x+\mathrm{i}y,z=x+iy,
(4)

where xxx and yyy are still ordinary real numbers. In terms of amplitude and phase, the same number is

z=r(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ),r=∣z∣=x2+y2,θ=atan2⁡(y,x).z=r(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta), \qquad r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}, \qquad \theta=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x).z=r(cosθ+isinθ),r=∣z∣=x2+y2​,θ=atan2(y,x).
(5)

So far, this notation only stores an arrow and allows arrows to be added. We still need an operation that advances phase. Let

R(θ)=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θR(\theta)=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\thetaR(θ)=cosθ+isinθ
(6)

denote a unit-amplitude phase factor. We want multiplication by R(θ)R(\theta)R(θ) to rotate an arrow through the angle θ\thetaθ. Successive phase advances must compose according to

R(θ1)R(θ2)=R(θ1+θ2).R(\theta_1)R(\theta_2)=R(\theta_1+\theta_2).R(θ1​)R(θ2​)=R(θ1​+θ2​).
(7)

This is the group property that makes phase evolution simple: performing one rotation and then another produces a rotation whose angle is the sum of the two angles.

Now consider a quarter-turn. The unit arrow at angle π/2\pi/2π/2 is

R(π/2)=i.R(\pi/2)=\mathrm{i}.R(π/2)=i.
(8)

Applying two quarter-turns must give a half-turn, whose unit arrow is -1. The desired composition rule therefore requires

i2=R(π/2)R(π/2)=R(π)=−1.\mathrm{i}^2 =R(\pi/2)R(\pi/2) =R(\pi) =-1.i2=R(π/2)R(π/2)=R(π)=−1.
(9)

Thus i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1 is not an arbitrary algebraic rule. It records the geometric fact that two quarter-turns make a half-turn. A complex number now has two related roles: it represents an arrow carrying amplitude and phase, and multiplication by it performs a scaling and a rotation.

Later we will discover the compact notation R(θ)=eiθR(\theta)=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}R(θ)=eiθ from the Taylor series rather than assume it at the beginning. Phase is defined modulo 2π2\pi2π and is undefined at z=0z=0z=0; atan2⁡\operatorname{atan2}atan2 selects one principal representative.

Complex conjugation reverses the phase:

zˉ=z∗=x−iy=r(cos⁡θ−isin⁡θ).\bar z=z^*=x-\mathrm{i}y=r(\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta).zˉ=z∗=x−iy=r(cosθ−isinθ).
(10)

The notations zˉ\bar zzˉ and z∗z^*z∗ mean the same thing. Mathematics often uses the bar, while chemistry and physics frequently use the star.

Geometrically, conjugation reflects a point across the real axis. It leaves the magnitude fixed, and

zz∗=∣z∣2.zz^*=|z|^2.zz∗=∣z∣2.
(11)

Complex arithmetic is geometry¶

The coordinate packaging preserves the vector addition introduced above:

(x1+iy1)+(x2+iy2)=(x1+x2)+i(y1+y2).(x_1+\mathrm{i}y_1)+(x_2+\mathrm{i}y_2) =(x_1+x_2)+\mathrm{i}(y_1+y_2).(x1​+iy1​)+(x2​+iy2​)=(x1​+x2​)+i(y1​+y2​).
(12)

Multiplication has a different geometric meaning. We can now verify the desired rotation rule algebraically. Using i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1 and the angle-addition formulas gives

R(θ1)R(θ2)=R(θ1+θ2).R(\theta_1)R(\theta_2)=R(\theta_1+\theta_2).R(θ1​)R(θ2​)=R(θ1​+θ2​).
(13)

This is exactly the rule we wanted: composing rotations adds their angles. For general complex numbers z1=r1R(θ1)z_1=r_1R(\theta_1)z1​=r1​R(θ1​) and z2=r2R(θ2)z_2=r_2R(\theta_2)z2​=r2​R(θ2​),

z1z2=r1r2R(θ1+θ2).z_1z_2=r_1r_2R(\theta_1+\theta_2).z1​z2​=r1​r2​R(θ1​+θ2​).
(14)

Multiplication therefore multiplies magnitudes and adds phases. One complex multiplication performs a scaling and a rotation. For example,

1+i=2 R(π/4),1+\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{2}\,R(\pi/4),1+i=2​R(π/4),
(15)

so multiplication by 1+i1+\mathrm{i}1+i scales by 2\sqrt{2}2​ and rotates by one eighth of a complete turn.

Division reverses these operations:

z1z2=r1r2R(θ1−θ2).\frac{z_1}{z_2} =\frac{r_1}{r_2}R(\theta_1-\theta_2).z2​z1​​=r2​r1​​R(θ1​−θ2​).
(16)

This requires z2≠0z_2\neq0z2​=0. Division divides the magnitudes and subtracts the phases. This is why polar form is usually the convenient form for multiplication, division, powers, and roots, while Cartesian form is usually convenient for addition.

At this point we have already built a group. A group is a collection of operations that can be composed without leaving the collection, with an identity operation and an inverse for every operation. Rotation also satisfies the remaining group requirement, associativity: it does not matter how we parenthesize a sequence of rotations. Here, rotating by θ1\theta_1θ1​ and then by θ2\theta_2θ2​ produces another rotation, rotating by θ1+θ2\theta_1+\theta_2θ1​+θ2​; doing nothing is the identity R(0)R(0)R(0); and R(−θ)R(-\theta)R(−θ) undoes R(θ)R(\theta)R(θ).

The unit complex numbers form the group U(1)U(1)U(1), while rotations of the plane form SO(2)SO(2)SO(2). The name SO(2)SO(2)SO(2) means the special orthogonal group in two dimensions: its matrices preserve lengths and angles and have determinant +1, so they describe rotations rather than reflections. These two groups are isomorphic: they are different mathematical objects with the same composition structure. The correspondence is

cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ⟷(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ).\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.cosθ+isinθ⟷(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​).
(17)

Multiplication by the complex number on the left performs the same rotation as multiplication by the matrix on the right. This provides a bridge to our next topic, linear algebra. We do not need the group names to calculate, but they show that complex phase is part of a broader mathematical structure rather than an isolated trick. The same idea—identify transformations and study how they compose—is the starting point for using symmetry throughout physics and chemistry.

Euler’s formula and phase¶

We can derive Euler’s formula directly from the Taylor series that we used in the previous lecture:

eiθ=1+iθ+(iθ)22!+(iθ)33!+⋯ .e^{\mathrm{i}\theta} =1+\mathrm{i}\theta+ \frac{(\mathrm{i}\theta)^2}{2!}+ \frac{(\mathrm{i}\theta)^3}{3!}+\cdots.eiθ=1+iθ+2!(iθ)2​+3!(iθ)3​+⋯.
(18)

Separating the even and odd powers gives

eiθ=(1−θ22!+θ44!−⋯ )+i(θ−θ33!+θ55!−⋯ )=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ.\begin{aligned} e^{\mathrm{i}\theta} &=\left(1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+\frac{\theta^4}{4!}-\cdots\right) \\ &\quad+\mathrm{i}\left(\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!} +\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\cdots\right) \\ &=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta. \end{aligned}eiθ​=(1−2!θ2​+4!θ4​−⋯)+i(θ−3!θ3​+5!θ5​−⋯)=cosθ+isinθ.​
(19)

The exponential eiθe^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ is therefore a point rotating around the unit circle. Its real projection is cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ and its imaginary projection is sin⁡θ\sin\thetasinθ.

We can now identify the rotation we introduced geometrically with the complex exponential:

R(θ)=eiθ,z=reiθ.R(\theta)=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}, \qquad z=re^{\mathrm{i}\theta}.R(θ)=eiθ,z=reiθ.
(20)

The exponential notation makes the group rule almost automatic:

eiθ1eiθ2=ei(θ1+θ2).e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_1}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_2} =e^{\mathrm{i}(\theta_1+\theta_2)}.eiθ1​eiθ2​=ei(θ1​+θ2​).
(21)

The Taylor series does more than prove an identity. As we keep more terms, its trajectory in the complex plane approaches the unit circle.

Euler’s formula also lets us solve for the trigonometric functions:

cos⁡z=eiz+e−iz2,sin⁡z=eiz−e−iz2i.\cos z=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}z}+e^{-\mathrm{i}z}}{2}, \qquad \sin z=\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}z}-e^{-\mathrm{i}z}}{2\mathrm{i}}.cosz=2eiz+e−iz​,sinz=2ieiz−e−iz​.
(22)

These formulas are useful, but our main point today is the phase: multiplying by eiθe^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ rotates an amplitude, and multiplying two such factors adds their phases.

Complex waves and beating¶

We now return to the wave that motivated the complex plane. A real traveling wave can be written as

E(x,t)=E0cos⁡(kx−ωt+ϕ).E(x,t)=E_0\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi).E(x,t)=E0​cos(kx−ωt+ϕ).
(23)

We represent it by the complex wave

E~(x,t)=E0ei(kx−ωt+ϕ),\widetilde E(x,t)=E_0e^{\mathrm{i}(kx-\omega t+\phi)},E(x,t)=E0​ei(kx−ωt+ϕ),
(24)

and remember that the physical field is its real part:

E(x,t)=Re⁡E~(x,t).E(x,t)=\operatorname{Re}\widetilde E(x,t).E(x,t)=ReE(x,t).
(25)

The complex representation is a calculation tool. Differentiation becomes multiplication,

∂E~∂x=ikE~,∂E~∂t=−iωE~,\frac{\partial \widetilde E}{\partial x} =\mathrm{i}k\widetilde E, \qquad \frac{\partial \widetilde E}{\partial t} =-\mathrm{i}\omega\widetilde E,∂x∂E​=ikE,∂t∂E​=−iωE,
(26)

and we can add complex waves first and take the real part only at the end. Much of physics is written in terms of complex amplitudes because phases compose in exactly this way.

Beating is a simple example. Add two waves with nearby angular frequencies:

e−iω1t+e−iω2t=2e−iωˉtcos⁡(Δω t2),e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_1t}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_2t} =2e^{-\mathrm{i}\bar\omega t} \cos\left(\frac{\Delta\omega\,t}{2}\right),e−iω1​t+e−iω2​t=2e−iωˉtcos(2Δωt​),
(27)

where

ωˉ=ω1+ω22,Δω=ω1−ω2.\bar\omega=\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}{2}, \qquad \Delta\omega=\omega_1-\omega_2.ωˉ=2ω1​+ω2​​,Δω=ω1​−ω2​.
(28)

The factor e−iωˉte^{-\mathrm{i}\bar\omega t}e−iωˉt gives the fast oscillation, while the cosine gives the slowly varying envelope. This is the beat signal we hear from nearby musical frequencies or measure when two lasers illuminate the same detector.

The optical field oscillates at the two original frequencies. A square-law detector instead measures the magnitude squared, which contains their difference frequency:

∣e−iω1t+e−iω2t∣2=2+2cos⁡(Δωt).\left|e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_1t}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_2t}\right|^2 =2+2\cos(\Delta\omega t).∣∣​e−iω1​t+e−iω2​t∣∣​2=2+2cos(Δωt).
(29)

Thus the detected beat angular frequency is ∣Δω∣|\Delta\omega|∣Δω∣. This distinction between the field and its measured intensity will matter again below.

Intensity and interference¶

A detector usually cannot follow the instantaneous oscillation of an optical field. It measures an average over many cycles. For

E(t)=E0cos⁡(ωt+ϕ),E(t)=E_0\cos(\omega t+\phi),E(t)=E0​cos(ωt+ϕ),
(30)

the average over one period is

⟨E(t)2⟩=1T∫0TE02cos⁡2(ωt+ϕ) dt=E022.\begin{aligned} \left\langle E(t)^2\right\rangle &=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T E_0^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi)\,dt \\ &=\frac{E_0^2}{2}. \end{aligned}⟨E(t)2⟩​=T1​∫0T​E02​cos2(ωt+ϕ)dt=2E02​​.​
(31)

The corresponding complex wave has constant squared magnitude,

∣E~(t)∣2=E~(t)E~(t)∗=E02.|\widetilde E(t)|^2 =\widetilde E(t)\widetilde E(t)^*=E_0^2.∣E(t)∣2=E(t)E(t)∗=E02​.
(32)

Therefore, when E0E_0E0​ is the peak amplitude,

⟨E(t)2⟩=12∣E~(t)∣2.\boxed{ \left\langle E(t)^2\right\rangle =\frac{1}{2}|\widetilde E(t)|^2 }.⟨E(t)2⟩=21​∣E(t)∣2​.
(33)

For a plane electromagnetic wave this gives

⟨I⟩=12ϵ0c∣E~0∣2.\left\langle I\right\rangle =\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0c|\widetilde E_0|^2.⟨I⟩=21​ϵ0​c∣E0​∣2.
(34)

Now let two fields have the same frequency but a relative phase ϕ\phiϕ:

E~1=E01ei(kx−ωt),E~2=E02ei(kx−ωt+ϕ).\widetilde E_1=E_{01}e^{\mathrm{i}(kx-\omega t)}, \qquad \widetilde E_2=E_{02}e^{\mathrm{i}(kx-\omega t+\phi)}.E1​=E01​ei(kx−ωt),E2​=E02​ei(kx−ωt+ϕ).
(35)

Their intensity is proportional to the squared magnitude of the total complex amplitude:

For the equal-amplitude plot below, I0I_0I0​ is the intensity of either field alone, so the normalization makes the constructive maximum I=4I0I=4I_0I=4I0​ and the destructive minimum zero.

I∝∣E~1+E~2∣2=E012+E022+2E01E02cos⁡ϕ.\begin{aligned} I&\propto|\widetilde E_1+\widetilde E_2|^2 \\ &=E_{01}^2+E_{02}^2+2E_{01}E_{02}\cos\phi. \end{aligned}I​∝∣E1​+E2​∣2=E012​+E022​+2E01​E02​cosϕ.​
(36)

What the plot shows. The left panel shows the rapid oscillation of the squared real field around its cycle average. The right panel shows how the normalized intensity of two equal fields changes continuously from constructive to destructive interference as their relative phase changes.

When ϕ=0\phi=0ϕ=0, the fields interfere constructively. When the amplitudes are equal and ϕ=π\phi=\piϕ=π, they interfere destructively. In a double-slit experiment, the two path lengths produce a phase difference

Δϕ=k(r2−r1),\Delta\phi=k(r_2-r_1),Δϕ=k(r2​−r1​),
(37)

so the bright and dark fringes are a spatial map of relative phase.

This exposes the central physical distinction between global and relative phase. Multiplying the entire field by a common phase does not change any intensity:

∣eiα(E01+E02eiϕ)∣2=∣E01+E02eiϕ∣2.\left|e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha} \left(E_{01}+E_{02}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\right)\right|^2 =\left|E_{01}+E_{02}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\right|^2.∣∣​eiα(E01​+E02​eiϕ)∣∣​2=∣∣​E01​+E02​eiϕ∣∣​2.
(38)

The global phase α\alphaα cancels, but the relative phase ϕ\phiϕ remains and controls the interference. Phase becomes observable through comparisons.
