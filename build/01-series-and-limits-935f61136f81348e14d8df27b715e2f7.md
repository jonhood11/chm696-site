## Learning goals

After this lecture, you should be able to:

- define convergence in terms of partial sums;
- apply the preliminary, comparison, integral, ratio, limit-comparison, and
  alternating-series tests;
- determine the radius and interval of convergence of a power series;
- construct Taylor and Maclaurin series; and
- estimate the error introduced by truncating a series.

## Why series?

Series are used throughout mathematics, chemistry, and physics to:

- represent functions using power, Taylor, and Fourier series;
- construct approximate solutions to differential equations and integrals;
- evaluate functions numerically; and
- represent functions in a finite basis for computation.

The central questions are:

- Do the partial sums approach a finite value? If so, the series
  **converges**; otherwise, it **diverges**.
- If a series contains positive and negative terms, does it converge
  absolutely or only conditionally?
- For a power series, what are its radius and interval of convergence?
- When a convergent series is truncated, how large is the resulting error?

## Infinite series and partial sums

An infinite series is a formal sum of infinitely many terms:

```{math}
S=a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n.
```

Its $N$-th **partial sum** is

```{math}
S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n.
```

The series converges to $S$ when the partial sums have the finite limit

```{math}
S=\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N.
```

If this finite limit does not exist, the series diverges. A divergent series
may grow without bound, oscillate, or fail to settle to a single value in some
other way.

The upper row below shows the **terms** $a_n$; the lower row shows the
corresponding **partial sums** $S_N$. Convergence is a statement about the
lower row, not merely about whether the terms in the upper row approach zero.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
n = np.arange(1, 31)
series_examples = [
    ("Geometric", 0.6 ** (n - 1), 1 / (1 - 0.6)),
    ("Harmonic", 1 / n, None),
    ("Alternating harmonic", (-1) ** (n - 1) / n, np.log(2)),
]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(11, 6), sharex="col")
for column, (title, terms, limit) in enumerate(series_examples):
    partial_sums = np.cumsum(terms)
    axes[0, column].stem(n, terms, linefmt=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD,
                         markerfmt="o", basefmt=" ")
    axes[0, column].set_title(title)
    axes[0, column].set_ylabel("$a_n$")
    axes[1, column].plot(n, partial_sums, "o-", color=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD,
                         markersize=3)
    if limit is not None:
        axes[1, column].axhline(limit, color=PURDUE_BLACK, linestyle="--",
                                linewidth=1.4, label=f"limit = {limit:.3f}")
        axes[1, column].legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8)
    axes[1, column].set(xlabel="$N$", ylabel="$S_N$")

fig.suptitle("Terms and partial sums have different convergence stories", y=1.01)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The geometric partial sums settle rapidly, the harmonic partial sums continue
to grow, and the alternating-harmonic partial sums approach their limit from
alternating sides.

## Geometric series

A finite geometric series with first term $a$ and common ratio $r$ is

```{math}
S_N=a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{N-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}ar^n.
```

For $r\neq1$, multiply by $r$ and subtract:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
S_N &= a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{N-1},\\
rS_N &= ar+ar^2+ar^3+\cdots+ar^N,\\
(1-r)S_N &= a(1-r^N).
\end{aligned}
```

Therefore,

```{math}
S_N=\frac{a(1-r^N)}{1-r}.
```

If $|r|<1$, then $r^N\to0$, and the infinite geometric series converges:

```{math}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}ar^n=\frac{a}{1-r},\qquad |r|<1.
```

For $|r|\geq1$ and $a\neq0$, the infinite geometric series does not
converge.

For $|r|<1$, truncating after $N$ terms leaves another, smaller geometric
series. The shaded bars visualize that remaining tail.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
r = 0.6
N_kept = 5
indices = np.arange(0, 15)
geometric_terms = r ** indices
tail_exact = r ** N_kept / (1 - r)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.8))
colors = [PURDUE_DARK_GOLD if k < N_kept else PURDUE_GOLD for k in indices]
ax.bar(indices, geometric_terms, color=colors, edgecolor="white")
ax.axvline(N_kept - 0.5, color=PURDUE_BLACK, linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
ax.text(1.4, 0.78, "terms retained", ha="center")
ax.text(8.0, 0.43,
        rf"remaining tail $=r^N/(1-r)={tail_exact:.4f}$",
        ha="center", color=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD)
ax.set(xlabel="$n$", ylabel="$r^n$",
       title=rf"A geometric series and its tail ($r={r}$, $N={N_kept}$)")
ax.set_xticks(indices[::2])
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Preliminary test for divergence

If $\sum a_n$ converges, then its terms must satisfy

```{math}
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0.
```

Consequently, if this limit is nonzero or does not exist, the series diverges.
The converse is false: $a_n\to0$ does not by itself prove convergence.

:::{admonition} Example

For the harmonic series, $a_n=1/n\to0$, so the preliminary test is
inconclusive. For

```{math}
\frac12+\frac23+\frac34+\frac45+\cdots,
```

the terms approach $1$, so the series diverges by the preliminary test.
:::

The harmonic series demonstrates why the converse fails. Its individual terms
approach zero, while its partial sums continue to grow approximately like
$\ln N$.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
n_harmonic = np.arange(1, 1001)
harmonic_terms = 1 / n_harmonic
harmonic_partial_sums = np.cumsum(harmonic_terms)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))
axes[0].plot(n_harmonic, harmonic_terms, color=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD, linewidth=2)
axes[0].set(xlabel="$n$", ylabel="$a_n=1/n$",
            title="The terms approach zero")

axes[1].plot(n_harmonic, harmonic_partial_sums, color=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD,

            title="The partial sums do not settle")

```

## Direct comparison test

Suppose the terms are nonnegative for all sufficiently large $n$.

- If $0\leq a_n\leq b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, then
  $\sum a_n$ converges.
- If $0\leq b_n\leq a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ diverges, then
  $\sum a_n$ diverges.

:::{warning}
The direction of the inequality matters. Being smaller than the terms of a
divergent series or larger than the terms of a convergent series gives no
conclusion.
:::

:::{admonition} Example

For $n\geq1$, $n!\geq2^{n-1}$, so

```{math}
0\leq\frac{1}{n!}\leq\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}.
```

Because $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-(n-1)}$ is a convergent geometric series,
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n!$ converges by direct comparison.
:::

## Integral test

Let $f(x)$ be continuous, positive, and decreasing for $x\geq N$, with
$a_n=f(n)$. Then

```{math}
\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}a_n
```

and

```{math}
\int_N^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
```

either both converge or both diverge.

:::{admonition} Example: the harmonic series

For $f(x)=1/x$,

```{math}
\int_1^B\frac{dx}{x}=\ln B.
```

Since $\ln B\to\infty$ as $B\to\infty$, the harmonic series diverges.
:::

For the decreasing function $f(x)=1/x$, each left-endpoint rectangle lies
above the curve. Extending the picture adds both rectangle area and integral
area without bound.


## Ratio test

For a series $\sum a_n$ with nonzero terms for sufficiently large $n$, let

```{math}
L=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|,
```

provided the limit exists. Then:

- if $L<1$, the series converges absolutely;
- if $L>1$, or $L=\infty$, the series diverges; and
- if $L=1$, the test is inconclusive.

:::{admonition} Example

For $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!$,

```{math}
\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|
=\frac{|x|}{n+1}\longrightarrow0.
```

The series therefore converges absolutely for every real or complex $x$.
:::

## Limit-comparison test

Suppose $a_n>0$, $b_n>0$, and

```{math}
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=c,
\qquad 0<c<\infty.
```

Then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ have the same convergence behavior.

:::{admonition} Example

For

```{math}
a_n=\frac{2n+1}{n^3+4},\qquad b_n=\frac{1}{n^2},
```

we have $a_n/b_n\to2$. Since $\sum 1/n^2$ converges, so does
$\sum a_n$.
:::

## Alternating series

An alternating series has the form

```{math}
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}b_n,
\qquad b_n\geq0.
```

If $b_{n+1}\leq b_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$ and $b_n\to0$, the
series converges.

A convergent series is **absolutely convergent** if $\sum |a_n|$ also
converges. A series that converges but does not converge absolutely is
**conditionally convergent**.

:::{note}
The alternating harmonic series converges conditionally. Ionic-lattice sums
provide a physical setting in which conditional convergence and the order or
geometry of summation require special care.
:::

For the alternating harmonic series, odd and even partial sums approach
$\ln 2$ from opposite sides. Consecutive partial sums bracket the exact value,
and the bracket width is the magnitude of the next term.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
n_alternating = np.arange(1, 17)
alternating_terms = (-1) ** (n_alternating - 1) / n_alternating
alternating_partial_sums = np.cumsum(alternating_terms)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.2))
ax.plot(n_alternating, alternating_partial_sums, color=PURDUE_GRAY,
        linewidth=1.2, alpha=0.8)
odd = n_alternating % 2 == 1
ax.scatter(n_alternating[odd], alternating_partial_sums[odd],
           color=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD, s=45, label="odd partial sums")
ax.scatter(n_alternating[~odd], alternating_partial_sums[~odd],
           color=PURDUE_GOLD, edgecolor=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD, s=45,
           label="even partial sums")
ax.axhline(np.log(2), color=PURDUE_BLACK, linestyle="--", linewidth=1.5,
           label=r"$\ln 2$")
ax.fill_between(n_alternating,
                np.minimum(alternating_partial_sums,
                           np.r_[alternating_partial_sums[1:], np.log(2)]),
                np.maximum(alternating_partial_sums,
                           np.r_[alternating_partial_sums[1:], np.log(2)]),
                color=PURDUE_GOLD, alpha=0.16)
ax.set(xlabel="$N$", ylabel="$S_N$",
       title="Alternating partial sums bracket the limiting value")
ax.set_xticks(n_alternating)
ax.legend(frameon=False, ncol=3, fontsize=8)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Operations on convergent series

- Multiplying every term by a fixed nonzero constant does not change whether a
  series converges, although it changes the sum.
- Changing finitely many terms does not change convergence or divergence,
  although it may change the sum.
- Two convergent series may be added or subtracted term by term, and their sums
  add or subtract correspondingly.
- The terms of an absolutely convergent series may be rearranged without
  changing its sum. This is not generally true for a conditionally convergent
  series.

## Power series

A power series centered at $x=a$ has the form

```{math}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(x-a)^n
=c_0+c_1(x-a)+c_2(x-a)^2+\cdots.
```

Every power series has a radius of convergence $R$, where
$0\leq R\leq\infty$, such that it:

- converges absolutely for $|x-a|<R$;
- diverges for $|x-a|>R$; and
- must be checked separately at the endpoints $x=a\pm R$ when $R$ is
  finite.

### Example: radius and interval of convergence

Consider

```{math}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-x)^n}{2^n}
=1-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x^2}{4}-\frac{x^3}{8}+\cdots.
```

The ratio test gives

```{math}
L=\left|\frac{x}{2}\right|.
```

Thus the radius of convergence is $R=2$. At $x=2$, the terms alternate
between $1$ and $-1$, so the series diverges. At $x=-2$, every term is
$1$, so the series also diverges. The interval of convergence is therefore
$(-2,2)$.

The radius determines the interior behavior, but it does not determine the
endpoints. Two series can have the same radius and different endpoint results.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(9, 3.8), sharex=True)
endpoint_cases = [
    (r"$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-x/2)^n$", "diverge", "diverge"),
    (r"$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(x/2)^n/n$", "converge", "diverge"),
]

for ax, (label, left_result, right_result) in zip(axes, endpoint_cases):
    ax.hlines(0, -3, -2, color="#b31b1b", linewidth=7, alpha=0.65)
    ax.hlines(0, -2, 2, color="#2e7d32", linewidth=7, alpha=0.7)
    ax.hlines(0, 2, 3, color="#b31b1b", linewidth=7, alpha=0.65)
    for location, result in [(-2, left_result), (2, right_result)]:
        if result == "converge":
            ax.scatter(location, 0, s=90, facecolor="#2e7d32",
                       edgecolor="white", linewidth=1.5, zorder=3)
        else:
            ax.scatter(location, 0, s=95, marker="x", color="#b31b1b",
                       linewidth=2.5, zorder=3)
        ax.text(location, 0.22, result, ha="center", fontsize=9)
    ax.text(-3.05, 0, label, ha="right", va="center")
    ax.set(ylim=(-0.32, 0.48), yticks=[])

axes[-1].set(xlim=(-3.2, 3.2), xticks=[-3, -2, 0, 2, 3], xlabel="$x$")
fig.suptitle("The interior follows the radius; endpoints require separate tests")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## Algebra and calculus of power series

Inside the radius of convergence:

- a power series may be differentiated or integrated term by term;
- power series may be added, subtracted, and multiplied; and
- one convergent power series may be substituted into another when the
  substituted values remain inside the outer series' convergence interval.

Term-by-term differentiation and integration preserve the **radius** of
convergence, but behavior at the endpoints must be checked again. A function's
power-series representation about a fixed center is unique wherever it exists.

## Taylor and Maclaurin series

The Taylor series of $f(x)$ about $x=a$ is

```{math}
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n,
```

when the series converges to $f(x)$. The special case $a=0$ is the
**Maclaurin series**:

```{math}
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}x^n.
```

For example, the derivatives of $\sin x$ cycle through
$\sin x,\cos x,-\sin x,-\cos x$. Evaluating at $x=0$ gives

```{math}
\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n\frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}.
```

## Maclaurin series to remember

```{math}
e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}
=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots,
\qquad -\infty<x<\infty.
```

```{math}
\sin x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n\frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!},
\qquad -\infty<x<\infty.
```

```{math}
\cos x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!},
\qquad -\infty<x<\infty.
```

```{math}
\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n,
\qquad |x|<1.
```

```{math}
\ln(1+x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\frac{x^n}{n}
=x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}-\cdots,
\qquad -1<x\leq1.
```

For a general real or complex exponent $p$,

```{math}
(1+x)^p
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{p}{n}x^n
=1+px+\frac{p(p-1)}{2!}x^2+\cdots,
\qquad |x|<1.
```

Endpoint behavior for the binomial series depends on $p$.

## How to construct power-series expansions

Useful approaches include:

1. Apply the Taylor formula by evaluating derivatives at the expansion point.
2. Add, multiply, divide, differentiate, or integrate known power series.
3. Substitute an expression into a known series while respecting its interval
   of convergence.

Examples worth practicing include $e^x\cos x$, $\tan x$, and
$1/(1-x)$. Since $\ln x$ is not analytic at $x=0$, expand it about
$x=1$ by writing

```{math}
\ln x=\ln\!\left(1+(x-1)\right).
```

## Taylor approximation and remainder

The degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial about $x=a$ is

```{math}
T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
```

The remainder is

```{math}
R_n(x)=f(x)-T_n(x).
```

Under the hypotheses of Taylor's theorem, there is a point $c$ between
$a$ and $x$ such that

```{math}
R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}.
```

If $|f^{(n+1)}(t)|\leq M$ between $a$ and $x$, then

```{math}
|R_n(x)|\leq\frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
```

The Taylor series converges to $f(x)$ at a point when $R_n(x)\to0$.

```{code-cell} python
exp_exact = np.exp(x_taylor)
taylor_orders = list(range(0, 15))
initial_order = 4

def exponential_taylor(x_values, order):
    approximation = np.zeros_like(x_values, dtype=float)
    for power in range(order + 1):
        approximation += x_values**power / math.factorial(power)
    return approximation

taylor_figure = make_subplots(
    rows=1,
    cols=2,
    subplot_titles=("Function and Taylor polynomial", "Absolute error and bound"),
    horizontal_spacing=0.12,
)
taylor_figure.add_trace(
    go.Scatter(x=x_taylor, y=exp_exact, mode="lines", name="$e^x$",
               line=dict(color="#000000", dash="dash", width=2.5)),
    row=1,
    col=1,
)

for order in taylor_orders:
    approximation = exponential_taylor(x_taylor, order)
    absolute_error = np.maximum(np.abs(exp_exact - approximation), 1e-16)
    derivative_bound = np.where(x_taylor >= 0, np.exp(x_taylor), 1.0)
    lagrange_bound = np.maximum(
        derivative_bound * np.abs(x_taylor) ** (order + 1)
        / math.factorial(order + 1),
        1e-16,
    )
    visible = order == initial_order
    taylor_figure.add_trace(
        go.Scatter(x=x_taylor, y=approximation, mode="lines",
                   name=f"$T_{{{order}}}(x)$", visible=visible,
                   line=dict(color="#8e6f3e", width=3)),
        row=1,
        col=1,
    )
    taylor_figure.add_trace(
        go.Scatter(x=x_taylor, y=absolute_error, mode="lines",
                   name="actual error", visible=visible,
                   line=dict(color="#8e6f3e", width=2.5)),
        row=1,
        col=2,
    )
    taylor_figure.add_trace(
        go.Scatter(x=x_taylor, y=lagrange_bound, mode="lines",
                   name="Lagrange bound", visible=visible,
                   line=dict(color="#6f727b", dash="dot", width=2)),
        row=1,
        col=2,
    )

taylor_steps = []
for order_index, order in enumerate(taylor_orders):
    visibility = [True] + [False] * (3 * len(taylor_orders))
    trace_start = 1 + 3 * order_index
    visibility[trace_start:trace_start + 3] = [True, True, True]
    taylor_steps.append(
        dict(
            label=str(order),
            method="update",
            args=[
                {"visible": visibility},
                {"title": f"Maclaurin approximation to e^x: order N = {order}"},
            ],
        )
    )

taylor_figure.update_layout(
    title=f"Maclaurin approximation to e^x: order N = {initial_order}",
    template="plotly_white",
    height=520,
    margin=dict(t=100),
    sliders=[
        dict(
            active=taylor_orders.index(initial_order),
            currentvalue={"prefix": "Polynomial order N: "},
            pad={"t": 35},
            steps=taylor_steps,
        )
    ],
    legend=dict(orientation="h", yanchor="bottom", y=1.02,
                xanchor="right", x=1),
)
taylor_figure.update_xaxes(title_text="$x$")
taylor_figure.update_yaxes(title_text="value", row=1, col=1)
taylor_figure.update_yaxes(title_text="absolute error", type="log", row=1, col=2)
taylor_figure.show()
```

the bound need not be tight to be valid.

## Error bounds for series

For an alternating series

```{math}
S=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k b_k
```

with $b_k\geq0$, $b_{k+1}\leq b_k$, and $b_k\to0$, the error after
terms through $k=N$ satisfies

```{math}
\left|S-\sum_{k=0}^{N}(-1)^k b_k\right|\leq b_{N+1}.
```

For a power series tail with coefficients satisfying
$|c_{n+1}|\leq|c_n|$ for $n\geq N+1$ and $|x|<1$, comparison with a
geometric series gives

```{math}
\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}c_nx^n\right|
\leq\frac{|c_{N+1}x^{N+1}|}{1-|x|}.
```

## Numerical uses and limits

Series can be used for numerical evaluation, differentiation, integration, and
limits. For example,

```{math}
\frac{\sin x}{x}
=1-\frac{x^2}{3!}+\frac{x^4}{5!}-\cdots,
```

so

```{math}
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1.
```

L'Hopital's rule provides an alternative for appropriate indeterminate forms,
but it requires its own hypotheses; it is not a consequence of this single
example.

### A direct-comparison envelope

The comparison $1/n!\leq 1/2^{n-1}$ becomes especially clear on a logarithmic
scale. The smaller positive series is trapped beneath a known convergent
geometric series.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
n_comparison = np.arange(1, 13)
factorial_terms = np.array([1 / math.factorial(int(k)) for k in n_comparison])
geometric_bound = 1 / 2 ** (n_comparison - 1)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.9))
ax.semilogy(n_comparison, factorial_terms, "o-", color=PURDUE_DARK_GOLD,
            linewidth=2.2, label="$1/n!$")
ax.semilogy(n_comparison, geometric_bound, "s--", color=PURDUE_BLACK,
            linewidth=1.7, label="$1/2^{n-1}$")
ax.fill_between(n_comparison, factorial_terms, geometric_bound,
                color=PURDUE_GOLD, alpha=0.25)
ax.set(xlabel="$n$", ylabel="term magnitude (log scale)",
       title="A convergent geometric series bounds $1/n!$ from above")
ax.set_xticks(n_comparison)
ax.legend(frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

### Approaching the radius of convergence

For $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-x/2)^n$, convergence becomes slower near
$|x|=2$ and fails outside that radius.

```{code-cell} python
:tags: [hide-input]
partial_sum_indices = np.arange(1, 36)
x_choices = [1.5, 1.9, 2.1]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.2))
for x_choice, color in zip(x_choices,
                           [PURDUE_DARK_GOLD, PURDUE_GOLD, PURDUE_GRAY]):
    powers = np.arange(0, len(partial_sum_indices))
    partial_sums = np.cumsum((-x_choice / 2) ** powers)
    ax.plot(partial_sum_indices, partial_sums, "o-", markersize=3,
            linewidth=1.7, color=color, label=rf"$x={x_choice}$")
    if abs(x_choice) < 2:
        ax.axhline(1 / (1 + x_choice / 2), color=color,
                   linestyle="--", linewidth=1, alpha=0.8)

ax.set(xlabel="$N$", ylabel="$S_N(x)$",
       title="Partial sums inside, near, and outside the radius $R=2$")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

## ChatGPT-assisted homework and project ideas

For any of these assignments, students may use ChatGPT as a provisional
collaborator, but the submission must include the important prompts, an
independent mathematical check, at least one correction or limitation, and the
student's own conceptual explanation. ChatGPT output by itself is not evidence.

1. **Convergence-test referee.** Ask ChatGPT to classify six carefully chosen
   series, including a boundary case, a conditionally convergent series, and a
   series whose terms approach zero but whose partial sums diverge. Check every
   classification from the definitions or a justified test, then write a
   referee report identifying the first unsupported step. The conceptual goal
   is to distinguish necessary conditions from sufficient tests.

2. **Design a deceptive series.** Use ChatGPT to propose a series that looks
   convergent from its first 50 partial sums but is actually divergent, or looks
   divergent initially but converges. Plot enough terms and partial sums to
   reveal the trap, prove the true behavior, and explain why finite numerical
   evidence cannot establish an infinite limiting claim.

3. **Taylor guarantee challenge.** Choose a physically meaningful function,
   such as a Boltzmann factor or a short-time exponential decay. Ask ChatGPT for
   a Taylor approximation that meets a specified accuracy on an interval.
   Verify the coefficients, test the claimed error numerically, and derive a
   remainder bound that actually guarantees the tolerance. Explain why the
   expansion point and interval matter.
