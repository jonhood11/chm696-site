## Gabor transform


- **Problem:** Although the Fourier transform provides detailed information about the frequency content of a given signal, it does not give any information about when in time those frequencies occur.

- For a signal with non-stationary frequency content, such as a musical composition, it is important to simultaneously characterize the frequency content and its evolution in time.

- The [Gabor transform]{style="color: blue"}, aka the short-time Fourier transform (STFT), computes a windowed FFT in a moving window.

- This STFT enables the localization of frequency content in time, resulting in the [spectrogram]{style="color: blue"}, which is a plot of frequency versus time.


:::{figure} figures/19-wavelets/Gabor1.png
:alt: Signal with frequency content that changes over time
:width: 90%

A global Fourier spectrum shows which frequencies occur but not when they occur.
:::

## Gabor transform (continued)


:::{figure} figures/19-wavelets/Gabor2.png
:alt: Moving-window construction of the short-time Fourier transform
:width: 90%

The STFT analyzes successive local windows of a nonstationary signal.
:::

The Gabor transform $G$ is given by:
```{math}
G_f(t,\omega)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
f(\tau)\,g^*(\tau-t)e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\tau
=\langle g_{t,\omega},f\rangle,
```
 where $g_{t,\omega}(\tau)$ is:
```{math}
g_{t,\omega}(\tau) = e^{i\omega \tau}g(\tau - t)
```
 The function $g(t)$ is the kernel, and is often chosen to be a Gaussian:
```{math}
g(s)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
```


The parameter $\sigma$ sets the window width, and $t$ is the center of the
moving window. A wider window improves frequency resolution but worsens time
resolution.

## Uncertainty principles

The time-frequency uncertainty principle:
```{math}
\Delta t^2
=\frac{\int (t-\bar t)^2|f(t)|^2\,dt}{\int|f(t)|^2\,dt},
\qquad
\Delta\omega^2
=\frac{\int(\omega-\bar\omega)^2|\hat f(\omega)|^2\,d\omega}
{\int|\hat f(\omega)|^2\,d\omega},
\qquad
\Delta t\,\Delta\omega\geq\frac{1}{2}.
```


Here $\bar t$ and $\bar\omega$ are the time and angular-frequency centroids.
The bound is written for the Fourier convention used in Lectures 17–18 and
does not require $f$ to be real.

In other words, a function $f(x)$ and its Fourier transform cannot both be arbitrarily localized.


## Wavelets


Wavelets exploit a multiresolution decomposition that provides different time
and frequency resolutions in different frequency bands.


:::{figure} figures/19-wavelets/wavelet1.png
:alt: Comparison of fixed STFT resolution with multiscale wavelet resolution
:width: 90%

Wavelets use short windows at high frequencies and wider windows at low frequencies.
:::

## Wavelets (continued)

Basis functions in wavelet decomposition are as follows:

- Start with mother wavelet $\psi(t)$

- Build a family of functions
```{math}
\psi_{a,b} (t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \psi \bigl( \frac{t-b}{a} \bigr)
```


- $a>0$ is the scale: small $a$ compresses the wavelet and probes higher
  frequencies, while large $a$ broadens it and probes lower frequencies

- $b$ translates the wavelet in time

Continuous [wavelet transform]{style="color: blue"}:
```{math}
W_{\psi}f(a,b)
=\langle\psi_{a,b},f\rangle
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_{a,b}^*(t)f(t)\,dt.
```


## Haar wavelet

The simplest and earliest example of a wavelet is the Haar wavelet, developed in 1910:
```{math}
\psi(t) =
\begin{cases}
  1, & 0 \le t < \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[6pt]
 -1, & \tfrac{1}{2} \le t < 1, \\[6pt]
  0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
```


:::{figure} figures/19-wavelets/wavelet2.png
:alt: Haar mother wavelet and translated or scaled copies
:width: 85%

The Haar wavelet is piecewise constant and has zero mean.
:::

## 2D wavelet image compression (continued)

The hierarchical representation of data in the wavelet transform is ideal for image compression.

Even with an aggressive truncation, retaining only 0.5$\%$ of the DWT coefficients, the coarse features of the image are retained.

If the transform concentrates signal energy into relatively few coefficients,
thresholding and encoding those coefficients can yield effective compression.
Which details are preserved depends on the wavelet, coefficient-selection rule,
and distortion metric; the largest coefficients are not universally the
\"most important\" for every task.


:::{figure} figures/19-wavelets/wavelet5.png
:alt: Image reconstruction after thresholding discrete wavelet coefficients
:width: 95%

Thresholding most wavelet coefficients can retain coarse image structure while discarding fine detail.
:::
