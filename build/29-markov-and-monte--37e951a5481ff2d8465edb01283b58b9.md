## Markov (Markovian) Process

A Markovian process (or Markov process) is a stochastic process in which the future evolution depends only on the present state, not on the history of how the system got there.

A stochastic process $\{X_t\}$ is **Markovian** if it satisfies the **Markov property**: $$P(X_{t+1} = x \mid X_t, X_{t-1}, \ldots, X_0)
= P(X_{t+1} = x \mid X_t).$$ where $P$ is the transition probability.

- **Memoryless**: future depends only on the present.

- Described by transition matrices (discrete process) or transition rates (continuous process).

- The system evolution is probabilistic, not deterministic.

- The process typically assumes short correlation times.


## Discrete Markov process

A discrete-time Markov process has states $$S = \{1,2,\dots,N\}.$$

[Transition matrix]{style="color: blue"} $\mathbf{P} = (P_{ij})$ is defined by:

- $P_{ij} \ge 0$,

- $\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^N P_{ij} = 1$ for every state $i$,

- $P_{ij} = \Pr[X_{t+1}=j \mid X_t=i]$ (probability of going from state $i$ to state $j$)

Each row of $\mathbf{P}$ is a probability distribution describing how the system evolves in one time step.


## Markov Property in Matrix Form

The current [probability distribution]{style="color: blue"} over states is a row vector: $$\boldsymbol{\pi}(t) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\Pr[X_t=1] & \cdots & \Pr[X_t=N]
\end{pmatrix}$$

Then the next-step distribution is: $$\boldsymbol{\pi}(t+1) = \boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \, \mathbf{P}.$$

After $n$ steps: $$\boldsymbol{\pi}(t+n) = \boldsymbol{\pi}(t) \, \mathbf{P}^n.$$

:::{note}
Interpretation: $\mathbf{P}^n$ gives $n$-step transition probabilities.
:::


## Example of a Transition Matrix

Consider a 3-state Markov chain. The transition matrix is:

$$\mathbf{P} =
\begin{pmatrix}
0.6 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\
0.2 & 0.5 & 0.3 \\
0.0 & 0.4 & 0.6
\end{pmatrix}.$$

Interpretation of the first row (transition probabilities for State $1$):

- Probability to stay: 0.6 stay,

- Probability to move to State $2$: 0.3,

- Probability to move to State $3$: 0.1.

Rows sum to 1, as required for a stochastic matrix.


## Two-Step and Multi-Step Transitions

The probability of being in state $j$ after two steps from $i$ is:

$$\mathbf{P}^2_{ij}
= \sum_{k=1}^N P_{ik} P_{kj}.$$

Thus:

- $\mathbf{P}^2$: all 2-step transition probabilities,

- $\mathbf{P}^3$: all 3-step transition probabilities,

- $\mathbf{P}^n$: $n$-step transition probabilities.

**Markov chains propagate by matrix powers**: $$\boldsymbol{\pi}(n) = \boldsymbol{\pi}(0) \mathbf{P}^n.$$


## Stationary Distribution

A stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}^*$ satisfies:

$$\boldsymbol{\pi}^* = \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \mathbf{P},
\qquad
\sum_i \pi^*_i = 1.$$

Intuitively, the stationary distribution provides the long-term probability of being in each state.

Properties:

- Represents the long-term behavior of the Markov chain.

- If chain is **irreducible** (i.e., all states communicate) and **aperiodic** (i.e., no deterministic cycles), then: $$\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbf{P}^n =
      \begin{pmatrix}
      \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \\
      \boldsymbol{\pi}^* \\
      \vdots \\
      \boldsymbol{\pi}^*
      \end{pmatrix}.$$

- The stationary distribution is unique under these conditions.


## 1D Random Walk as a Markov Process

A simple model of diffusion or stochastic motion:

$$X_{n+1} = X_n + \eta_n,$$ where $$\eta_n =
\begin{cases}
+1 & \text{with probability } p, \\
-1 & \text{with probability } q = 1-p.
\end{cases}$$

**Markov property:** $$P(X_{n+1} = x \mid X_n) = P(\eta_n = x-X_n).$$

Properties:

- Mean displacement: $\langle X_n \rangle = (p-q)\, n$.

- Variance: $\mathrm{Var}(X_n) = 4pq\, n$.

- In the continuum limit: leads to the diffusion equation.

Applications:

- molecular diffusion,

- polymer models,

- financial models (stock price steps)


## Monte-Carlo method

**Monte-Carlo (MC)** methods compute quantities using random sampling.

- Evaluate integrals in high dimensions.

- Sample probability distributions.

- Estimate thermodynamic and statistical averages.

Key idea: $$\langle A \rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} A(x_i),$$ where the $x_i$ are random samples.

## Monte-Carlo in molecular simulations


- The task is to explore the molecular configurational space $\mathbf{r}^N$ and evaluate average thermodynamic properties.

- Let's consider average potential energy as an example: $$\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \int d\mathbf{r}^N V(\mathbf{r}^N) \rho(\mathbf{r}^N)$$ $\rho(\mathbf{r}^N)$ is the probability of obtaining the configuration $\mathbf{r}^N$: $$\rho(\mathbf{r}^N) = \frac{\exp[-V(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{Z},$$ $Z$ is the configurational integral $$Z = \int d\mathbf{r}^N \exp \left( -\frac{V(\mathbf{r}^N)}{k_BT} \right)$$


## Monte-Carlo in molecular simulations (continued)

Let's try to calculate the potential energy integral using a simple Monte Carlo method. This will result in the following steps:

1.  Obtain a configuration of the system by randomly generating 3N Cartesian coordinates, which are assigned to the particles.

2.  Calculate the potential energy of the configuration, $V(\mathbf{r}^N)$.

3.  From the potential energy, calculate the Boltzmann factor, $\exp[-V(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]$.

4.  Add the Boltzmann factor to the accumulated sum of Boltzmann factors and the potential energy contribution to its accumulated sum and return to step 1.

5.  After a number, $N_{trial}$, of iterations, the mean value of the potential energy would be calculated using: $$\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}}  V_i(\mathbf{r}^N) \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}}  \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}$$


## Monte-Carlo in molecular simulations (continued)

$$\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}}  V_i(\mathbf{r}^N) \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}}  \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}$$

**Problem:** The large number (most!) of configurations have extremely small (effectively zero) Boltzmann factors caused by high-energy overlaps between the particles. Only a very small proportion of the phase space corresponds to low-energy configurations where there are no overlapping particles and where the Boltzmann factor has an appreciable value. These low-energy regions coincide with the physically observed phases such as solid, liquid, etc.

**Solution:**

- Let's try to generate configurations that make a large contribution to the integral.

- This is the strategy adopted in importance sampling, which is the essence of the Metropolis Monte Carlo introduced in 1953.


## Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC)

MCMC constructs a **Markov chain** whose stationary distribution is the target distribution $\pi(x)$. Typical scheme:

1.  From current state $x$, propose a move $x'$.

2.  Accept or reject this move based on transition rules.

3.  Repeat to generate samples.

The chain must be:

- [Ergodic]{style="color: blue"}

- Aperiodic

- Satisfy [detailed balance]{style="color: blue"}

$$\pi(x) P(x \to x') = \pi(x') P(x' \to x).$$


## Ergodicity in Markov Chains

A Markov chain is [ergodic]{style="color: blue"} if it satisfies:

1.  **Irreducibility**: every state can be reached from any other state,

2.  **Aperiodicity**: the system does not get trapped in deterministic cycles

3.  **Positive recurrence**: expected return time to each state is finite.

**Consequences of ergodicity:**

- A unique stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}^*$ exists.

- [Long-term time averages equal ensemble averages]{style="color: blue"}: $$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n f(X_k)
      = \sum_i \pi^*_i f(i) = \langle f \rangle.$$

- In a continuous-time ergodic Markov process X(t), this becomes: $$\lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^\infty f(X(T))dt = \int f(x) \pi(x) dx = \langle f \rangle$$

- MCMC simulations converge to the correct equilibrium distribution.


## Detailed Balance: Intuition

**Key Question:** How can we ensure that a Markov chain samples from a desired distribution $\pi$?

**Idea:** In equilibrium, probability flow between any two states is symmetric:

$$\text{Flow}(i \to j) = \text{Flow}(j \to i)$$

**Interpretation:**

- At equilibrium, every microscopic transition is balanced by its reverse transition.

- No net probability currents.

- System appears ["reversible"]{style="color: blue"} at the level of state transitions.

**Consequence:** If this condition holds, $\pi$ is the stationary distribution of the chain.


## Formal Definition of Detailed Balance

A Markov chain with transition matrix $P$ satisfies **detailed balance** with respect to distribution $\pi$ if

$$\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}
\quad \forall i,j.$$

**Meaning:**

- $\pi_i P_{ij}$ = probability of being in state $i$ and moving to $j$.

- $\pi_j P_{ji}$ = probability of being in $j$ and moving to $i$.

- Equality ensures reversible microscopic dynamics.

This is a stronger condition than merely having $\pi \mathbf{P} = \pi$.


## Detailed Balance Implies Stationarity

Given detailed balance: $$\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji},$$

Sum over all $i$: $$\sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j \sum_i P_{ji}.$$

But since columns of $P$ sum to 1: $\sum_i P_{ji} = 1$,\
we get: $$\sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j.$$

Therefore: $$\pi \mathbf{P} = \pi,$$ so $\pi$ is the stationary distribution.

**Conclusion:** A reversible chain automatically has the correct equilibrium distribution.


## Reversibility and Microscopic Interpretation

Detailed balance is equivalent to [time reversibility]{style="color: blue"}:

$$\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}$$

means that the joint probability of observing the transition $$i \to j$$ is the same as observing $$j \to i.$$

**Physical interpretation (statistical mechanics):**

- Detailed balance corresponds to **microscopic reversibility**.

- In equilibrium, every elementary process is balanced by its reverse.

- No net entropy production at the transition level.


## Visual Interpretation


**Detailed balance = equality of flows**

$$\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}.$$

This condition prevents probability currents from circulating and ensures reversibility.


## Detailed Balance and Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Most Monte Carlo algorithms explicitly enforce detailed balance so that $\pi$ is the correct sampling distribution.

:::{note}
Examples:

- **Metropolis Algorithm** Ensures $$\pi_i q_{ij} a_{ij} = \pi_j q_{ji} a_{ji}$$ where $q_{ij}$ is proposal probability and $a_{ij}$ acceptance probability.

- **Metropolis--Hastings** updates are *constructed* to satisfy detailed balance.

- **Gibbs sampling** automatically satisfies detailed balance.

- **Replica exchange MC/MD** satisfies detailed balance across temperatures.
:::


## When Detailed Balance is Not Required

Although very common, detailed balance is **not necessary** for correct sampling. The weaker condition is:

$$\pi \mathbf{P} = \pi.$$

**Why avoid detailed balance?**

- Non-reversible chains often mix faster.

- Can reduce autocorrelation times in MCMC.

- Used in modern algorithms (e.g., lifted MCMC, non-reversible Langevin).

**Takeaway:** Detailed balance is convenient, intuitive, and widely used---but not mandatory.


## Metropolis Monte Carlo

In Metropolis Monte Carlo:

$$P_{ij} = q(i \to j)\, a(i \to j), \quad i \ne j,$$ where:

- $q(i \to j)$: proposal distribution,

- $a(i \to j)$: Metropolis acceptance probability, $$a(i \to j)
  = \min\left(1,\frac{\pi(j) q(j\to i)}{\pi(i) q(i \to j)}\right).$$

Diagonal elements: $$P_{ii} = 1 - \sum_{j\ne i} P_{ij}.$$

Guarantees:

- rows sum to 1,

- detailed balance,

- convergence to target distribution $\pi$.


## Metropolis Algorithm: Step-by-Step

Given a target probability distribution $\pi(x) \propto e^{-\beta E(x)}$ (e.g., Boltzmann weight):

1.  **Start** from an initial state $x$.

2.  **Propose** a new state $x'$ from a proposal distribution $q(x \to x')$.

3.  **Compute** the Metropolis acceptance ratio: $$\alpha = \min\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(x') q(x' \to x)}{\pi(x) q(x \to x')}\right).$$

4.  **Accept** the new state with probability $\alpha$: $$x_{n+1} =
        \begin{cases}
        x', & \text{with probability } \alpha,\\
        x, & \text{otherwise}.
        \end{cases}$$

5.  **Iterate** to generate a correlated trajectory sampling $\pi(x)$.

**Effect:**

- Energy downhill moves (toward higher probability) are always accepted.

- Energy uphill moves are accepted with the appropriate probability.


## Metropolis Monte-Carlo in Molecular Modeling

Again, we are up to computing this $3N$-dimensional integral: $$\langle V(\mathbf{r}^N)\rangle = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}}  V_i(\mathbf{r}^N) \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{trial}}  \exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]}$$

- The crucial feature of the Metropolis approach is that it biases the generation of configurations towards those that make the most significant contribution to the integral.

- Specifically, it generates states with a probability $\exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]$ and then counts each of them equally.

- By contrast, the simple Monte Carlo integration method generates states with equal probability (both high- and low-energy) and then assigns them a weight $\exp[-V_i(\mathbf{r}^N)/k_B T]$.

- In this way, Metropolis MC avoids explicit integration over $3N$-dimensional space.


## Metropolis MC: Proposal and Acceptance


:::{admonition} Center
:::

**Acceptance probability:** $$A(i \to j) = \min\Bigg(1, \frac{\pi(j)\, q(j \to i)}{\pi(i)\, q(i \to j)} \Bigg)$$

**Explanation:**

- $\pi(i)$ = target probability (e.g., Boltzmann factor $e^{-\beta E_i}$)

- $q(i \to j)$ = probability of proposing $j$ from $i$

- If accepted: move to $j$, otherwise stay at $i$

- Ensures **detailed balance** and convergence to $\pi$


## Proposal Distributions

The proposal distribution $q(i \to j)$ determines how new states are suggested in Metropolis MC:

- **Symmetric proposals:**

  - $q(i \to j) = q(j \to i)$

  - Example: uniform step to a neighbor

  - Acceptance ratio simplifies to $A = \min(1, \pi(j)/\pi(i))$

- **Asymmetric proposals:**

  - $q(i \to j) \neq q(j \to i)$

  - Example: biased random walk toward higher probability regions

  - Acceptance ratio includes $q(j \to i)/q(i \to j)$ factor

- **Discrete proposals:**

  - Pick a new state randomly from a finite set

  - Example: random change of a single atom coordinate

- **Problem-specific proposals:**

  - Use system knowledge to propose moves efficiently

  - Example: block updates, cluster moves, Hamiltonian proposals

**Goal:** Choose $q$ to explore the state space efficiently while ensuring detailed balance.
